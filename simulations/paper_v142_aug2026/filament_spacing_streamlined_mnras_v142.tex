\begin{abstract}
Dense cores form along the filaments that thread star-forming clouds. If they come from the classical gravitational fragmentation of a cylinder they should be separated by about five to six filament widths. We re-examine that expectation in four \textit{Herschel} Gould Belt Survey clouds using \textit{Gaia} Data Release 3 distances.

The median of all pairwise separations cannot measure a local spacing: it converges on a fixed fraction of the region's length. What a one-fragment-per-wavelength theory predicts is the filament length per core, and on that quantity two of our four clouds agree with the classical value while two fall below it. Neighbouring-core separations, measured only where cores occur, are several times shorter, though its size is set by the structural definition adopted, changing by a factor of two between a persistent-crest and a medial-axis network. The gap between them is the measurement: most of the crest is bare, so cores sit in tight groups separated by long empty stretches.

Restricting to supercritical crest brings the two together, but that criterion is partly endogenous and after correction only two clouds retain enough cores to decide. A deficit of the smallest separations is indistinguishable from the extraction resolution limit.

Simulations show a contracting filament need not fragment at the equilibrium wavelength, and that mode selection tracks the density at which the modes grow, but they resolve no growth-rate maximum and give only an upper bound: a counterexample, not an explanation. With four clouds these are sample statements rather than a population law.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The \textit{Herschel} Space Observatory established the ubiquity of filamentary structure in nearby star-forming clouds \citep{Andre2010} and a characteristic width of $\sim$0.1\,pc across diverse environments \citep{Arzoumanian2011}, though the universality and physical interpretation of that scale remain contested \citep{Panopoulou2017,Panopoulou2022}. For an isothermal cylinder the critical mass per unit length is $M_{\rm line,crit}\approx16\,M_\odot\,{\rm pc^{-1}}$ \citep{Ostriker1964}, and a marginally-stable cylinder fragments at $\lambda_{\rm m}\approx5$--$6\times$FWHM \citep{Inutsuka1992,Fischera2012}. Observed core separations are widely reported to fall below that value, and the deficit has been read as evidence for additional physics.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{What this paper does.} We re-measure core spacing along filament crests in four HGBS clouds with \textit{Gaia} DR3 distances \citep{Zhang2023}, and argue that the apparent conflict with classical theory is largely a question of which statistic is compared with which prediction.

Two observational points anchor the paper. First, the all-pairs (pairwise) median separation is not an estimator of local spacing at all. It converges to $\approx0.293$ times the extent of whatever segment it is applied to, so it measures the region selected (Section~\ref{sec:statistics}). We therefore use adjacent along-crest separations for local spacing and retain the all-pairs statistic only for historical comparison. We make no claim that published HGBS spacings generally suffer from this: the values we could check are local or along-fibre statistics that agree with ours (Table~\ref{tab:literature}).

Second, and more importantly, the local and the global statistics give different answers, and that difference is the result. Measured only within core-bearing stretches, adjacent separations are several times shorter than $\lambda_{\rm m}$ under every skeleton and width convention we tried. Measured over the whole crest, the filament length per core is close to classical in two of the four clouds. The reconciliation is that cores occupy only $6$--$27$ per cent of the crest: they form in tight groups separated by long bare stretches. The scientific question therefore shifts from ``why is the fragmentation wavelength too short?'' to ``why is core formation spatially intermittent along the filament?''.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Candidate explanations.} Three mechanisms could shorten a local spacing. (i) \emph{Hierarchical fragmentation}, in which filaments are bundles of velocity-coherent fibres \citep{Hacar2013,Hacar2018,Yang2024}, so that measuring across the bundle compresses the apparent filament-level spacing. (ii) \emph{Magnetic tension} \citep{Nakamura1993}: only a helical or toroidal field shortens the axial mode, since a static longitudinal field leaves it unchanged and a perpendicular field lengthens it. (iii) \emph{Dynamical fragmentation}, in which a still-collapsing filament fragments at a scale set by its current contracting state rather than by the marginally-stable equilibrium mode; this is already qualitatively expected in the dynamical-ISM paradigm \citep{Andre2014,Clarke2016,Clarke2017,Clarke2020}.

The evidence here is not evenly distributed among them. We test (iii) directly, treat (ii) only with a geometric toy model, and do not test the force-balanced helical configuration at all, so (ii) is mentioned for completeness as an unconstrained alternative rather than as a coequal candidate. The data cannot discriminate between the three, and nothing below should be read as identifying the cause.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{New contributions.} These are three: a homogeneous, \textit{Gaia}-DR3, multi-convention measurement of adjacent-core spacing and filament length per core across the four primary clouds, together with the occupancy statistics that connect them; the identification of the region-extent bias in all-pairs separation statistics in this context (the underlying triangular-distribution order statistic is elementary and we claim no new mathematics); and a controlled numerical experiment in which the identical pipeline recovers the classical wavelength for a relaxed equilibrium cylinder but finds no resolved maximum for a contracting one, extended here to a five-point ladder in contraction rate.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Scope.} With four independent clouds these are sample statements, not a universal fragmentation law, and all $\pm$ values quoted in this paper are descriptive $N=4$ ranges rather than confidence intervals. Symbols are collected in Table~\ref{tab:symbols}.

\noindent\textbf{Notation.} Four normalised quantities carry the argument. Capital symbols denote ratios to the classical benchmark; $s_{\rm ACS,med}$ denotes a raw separation in parsecs.
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item $S_{\rm global}=(L/N)/W$, the unconditional filament length per core. \emph{This is the quantity a one-fragment-per-wavelength theory predicts}, and it is the primary comparison with $\lambda_{\rm m}/W\approx5$--$6$.
\item $S_{\rm local}=s_{\rm ACS,med}/W$, the median adjacent-core separation measured \emph{only within core-bearing stretches}: a descriptor of the interior of core-bearing regions, not a fragmentation wavelength.
\item $S_{\rm elig}=(L_{\rm elig}/N_{\rm elig})/W$, $S_{\rm global}$ restricted to filament above the critical line mass; an exploratory diagnostic (Section~\ref{sec:eligible}).
\item $S_{\rm sim}^{\rm upper}=\lambda_{\rm turn}/W$, an \emph{upper bound} from the simulations, never a measured wavelength.
\end{itemize}
The difference between $S_{\rm global}$ and $S_{\rm local}$ measures how unevenly cores occupy the crest. Every other ratio quoted here is a sensitivity test or alternative convention on one of these four, decoded in Table~\ref{tab:headline}.

\section{Observational analysis}

\subsection{The sample}

Our sample comprises 8 high-confidence HGBS regions (four \emph{primary}, Table~\ref{tab:sample}; four \emph{limited}, Appendix~\ref{sec:limited_sample}). The original catalogues \citep{Konyves2015, Konyves2020, Ladjelate2020, Pezzuto2021} adopted distances of $130$--$400$\,pc from the best estimates available in 2010--2015 (Table~\ref{tab:literature} lists the pre-Gaia distance and its source per region; note that \citealt{Konyves2020} already adopted $\sim400$\,pc for Orion~B); Gaia DR3 parallaxes \citep{Zhang2023} have since enabled substantial revisions, largest for the more distant regions, whose pre-Gaia distances were the most uncertain.

We adopt the Gaia DR3 YSO-based distances from \citet{Zhang2023} (Table~4), with typical uncertainties of 3\%--5\%. The revisions are strongly region-dependent (largest for Serpens $260\to458$\,pc, $+76\%$, and Aquila $260\to436$\,pc, $+68\%$; Orion~B, Taurus and Ophiuchus essentially unchanged); since spacings scale linearly with distance, the core-count-weighted mean rises only modestly ($\sim10\%$ for the primary regions), dominated by Aquila rather than being a uniform $+32\%$ shift. Because Gaia DR3 thus compresses or expands each cloud non-uniformly, the revision is not a single global rescaling, which is a further reason the clouds must be treated as $N=4$ independent units rather than by pooling cores that carry very different distance corrections. The observed spacing is a line-density statistic, not a resonance, and coincides with the theoretical wavelength only to order of magnitude.

The analysis includes all HGBS-identified cores (starless, prestellar and protostellar), extracted and classified within the consistent HGBS framework \citep{Konyves2015, Konyves2020}. Including unbound starless and protostellar sources may introduce some bias, but fragmentation produces a continuum of boundness states, and restricting the sample to prestellar cores would shrink several regions (notably TMC1, CRA and Serpens) below statistical usefulness. Core positions and properties are taken directly from the published catalogues.

Protostellar migration displacements of $0.01$--$0.05$\,pc \citep{Kirk2016, Mattern2018} are small against the $\sim0.2$--$0.3$\,pc spacing; we estimate the resulting ACS bias at $\sim5$--$10\%$ and carry it as a systematic (Section~\ref{sec:error_budget}).

\subsection{Measurement method}
\label{sec:methodology}

The analysis pipeline, from raw HGBS maps to the final characteristic $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W$, proceeds from the HGBS column-density maps and \textit{Gaia} DR3 distances through skeleton extraction, morphological bridging, core association within $0.06$\,pc of a spine, graph ordering along each component and adjacent-core separation. Filament skeletons were identified using DisPerSE \citep{Sousbie2011} following standard HGBS methodology \citep{Arzoumanian2011}, with a $3\sigma$ persistence threshold and manual editing restricted to removing obvious artefacts (e.g.\ spurs connecting unrelated structures). Its effect on the spacing is small ($\lesssim5\%$), bounded by the junction-retention and association-threshold sensitivity tests as a \emph{proxy} (Table~\ref{tab:errorbudget}). Because the skeleton algorithm is the dominant systematic in this paper, robustness to the persistence threshold of the adopted ontology is part of the measurement and not future work, and we have therefore tested it in two ways (Section~\ref{sec:persistence}). The manual editing itself is bounded only by these proxies and is carried as such in the error budget (Section~\ref{sec:error_budget}). Because skeletons and core positions derive from distance-independent angular positions, the Gaia DR3 revisions require no re-extraction. The measured spacing is insensitive to these choices: excluding junctions changes it by $<5\%$ and varying the association threshold between $1\times$ and $1.5\times$ the width by $<3\%$ (a coarse two-point check, distinct from the finer $0.04$--$0.08$\,pc half-width sweep of Section~\ref{sec:injection_recovery} that gives a $4$--$7\%$ per-region spread; the primary ACS pipeline associates cores within a half-width, $\sim0.06$\,pc, of a spine).

Core-to-core spacings were also measured using the pairwise median statistic, which Section~\ref{sec:statistics} shows converges to $\approx0.293L$ rather than the fragmentation wavelength for $N\gtrsim5$; we retain it only as a comparability statistic with prior HGBS work.

Because physical spacing is angular separation times distance, the $\lambda\propto d$ scaling is tautological and we do not present it as corroboration; the relevant check is that the \emph{residuals} about that scaling show no trend with the absolute revision fraction $|\Delta D/D_{\rm original}|$, the two most-revised clouds (Aquila, Serpens) do not stand out as having the largest or smallest residuals, so the revision does not preferentially bias the most-revised clouds. With only four clouds a formal correlation test has negligible power and we do not quote one. Two caveats remain. The YSO-based distances \citep{Zhang2023} are assumed to apply to the dense gas measured here, and the Aquila/Serpens Rift has substantial line-of-sight depth ($\sim$\,tens of pc in the 3-D dust reconstructions of \citealt{Zucker2020} and \citealt{Green2024}), so a single assigned distance may blend populations. We quantified the blending with a geometric Monte-Carlo model in which two crests at different depths are projected onto a common spine, taking an intrinsic spacing of $0.20$\,pc at $d=436$\,pc. The model draws each crest's line-of-sight offset from a Gaussian of dispersion $\sigma_z$, with the two crests separated by $2\sigma_z$ in depth, and we scan $\sigma_z=5$, $10$, $15$ and $30$\,pc (total separations of $10$--$60$\,pc, spanning the $\sim$\,tens of parsecs quoted by \citealt{Zucker2020} and \citealt{Green2024} for the Rift). Superposition interleaves the two chains and \emph{shortens} the measured median, to $0.41$--$0.49$ of the true value across that range, whereas the distance spread alone (which rescales each spacing by $d/(d+z)$) contributes only $\pm1$ to $\pm7$\,per cent. Blending therefore biases the ACS towards \emph{more} sub-classical values rather than less. The $\sim50$\,per cent figure is a worst case in which two crests overlap completely; the realistic effect is smaller because most associated cores lie on coherent crests. We carry it as a one-sided systematic in Table~\ref{tab:errorbudget}. As a floor, retaining the pre-Gaia distances lowers the primary-region characteristic to $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W\approx1.4$ (still sub-classical), so the sub-classical \emph{sign} does not depend on the distance revision, though its magnitude does.



The measurement convention, above all the skeleton algorithm, dominates the numerical value: the DisPerSE and FilFinder skeletons of the identical Orion~B map (Fig.~\ref{fig:skeleton_compare}) trace substantially different networks, moving the inferred $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W$ by a factor $\sim$2 while the sub-classical \emph{sign} holds for both (Section~\ref{sec:error_budget}).

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The four primary regions are the independent statistical units throughout. Individual cores and spacings within a cloud are not independent, and the nesting is propagated in the hierarchical bootstrap of Section~\ref{sec:error_budget}.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Two statistics, two different answers.} The adjacent-core spacing $s_{\rm ACS,med}$ is \emph{conditional}: measured only where cores are adjacent, it characterises the interior of core-bearing stretches and says nothing about filament that has formed no cores. The core line density $N/L$, quoted through its reciprocal $L/N$, is \emph{unconditional}, and it is this quantity that a one-fragment-per-wavelength theory predicts.

Taking the predicted quantity first, $S_{\rm global}=3.4$, $3.4$, $4.5$ and $5.8$ for Orion~B, Aquila, Taurus and Perseus. \emph{On the comparison classical theory actually makes, two of the four clouds are therefore not discrepant at all}, and the other two fall below the classical band by less than a factor of two. Conditionally, $S_{\rm local}$ is sub-classical by a factor $\approx2.4$--$4.3$ (Table~\ref{tab:headline}). The reconciliation is the occupancy result of Section~\ref{sec:intermittency}: cores occupy only $6$--$27$ per cent of the crest.

Every comparison of $S_{\rm local}$ with $5$--$6\,W$ below, including Fig.~\ref{fig:spacing}, is therefore a comparison between a conditional descriptor and a fragmentation wavelength. It is not a measurement of a shortened fragmentation wavelength and we do not use it as one. The sign of the local deficit is robust under every convention we tried; its magnitude is not, being dominated by the choice of skeleton (Section~\ref{sec:whatfilament}).

\subsection{The dominant observational systematic: filament width}
\label{sec:widths}

Two width conventions recur throughout: the \emph{fixed-width} convention (the canonical HGBS $W_{\rm fil}=0.10$\,pc for every region) and the \emph{region-Plummer} convention (a beam-deconvolved Plummer FWHM fitted per region); since $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W\propto1/W$, the broader region-specific widths give a \emph{lower} $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W$. The fixed $W_{\rm fil}=0.10$\,pc convention is the largest single lever on $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W$, comparable to the whole discrepancy, so we measured region-specific widths directly. Along each spine we sampled the column-density profile perpendicular to the local tangent and fitted a beam-deconvolved Plummer function (the HGBS width definition; \citealt{Arzoumanian2019}) to hundreds of profiles per region ($18.2''$ beam; Table~\ref{tab:widths}). All four regions give $p\simeq2.0$ and $W_{\rm fil}=0.11$--$0.16$\,pc, agreeing with the published HGBS values and slightly broader than $0.10$\,pc, so the region-specific $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W$ ($1.1$--$1.8$, median $\approx1.4$) is \emph{lower} than the raw fixed-width value ($\approx2.1$; Table~\ref{tab:headline}); every region is sub-classical under either convention. A Gaussian-crest estimate returns a much narrower Taurus width ($0.031$\,pc). We reject it on physical grounds rather than because of the ratio it implies: a Gaussian fits only the inner ridge and ignores the power-law wings that carry most of the column density, whereas the Plummer form models the wings and is the profile used to define the HGBS filament width. The Plummer fit ($0.11$\,pc, $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W\approx1.1$) makes Taurus the most sub-classical region. The result is thus not conditional on the width convention. We prefer the Plummer widths on physical grounds: they fit the observed $p\simeq2$ profile of HGBS filaments and are far less sensitive to the fitting radius than either a single fixed $0.10$\,pc value or a Gaussian inner-ridge fit.

\subsection{Sample classification and distance robustness}
\label{sec:distance_uncertainties}

We classify the eight regions (Table~\ref{tab:sample}; Appendix~\ref{sec:limited_sample}) as \textbf{primary} (Orion~B, Aquila, Perseus, Taurus) or \textbf{limited} by three criteria fixed \emph{a priori} (before the measurement): $N>500$ cores; a well-established distance; and a skeleton simple enough for unambiguous core-to-filament association. This selection is cross-checked by the leave-one-out test below. The limited sample fails at least one criterion (Ophiuchus on skeleton complexity, the others on small samples) and are excluded from the primary ACS measurement. The largest distance revisions (Serpens $+76\%$, Aquila $+68\%$) are independently corroborated by VLBI maser parallaxes (validating Gaia DR3 to $<5\%$; \citealt{Kounkel2018,Xu2021,OrtizLeon2018,OrtizLeon2017}), extinction estimates \citep{Yan2022,Green2024} and co-spatiality with Aquila \citep{Zucker2020}; Serpens is \emph{limited} and excluded in any case. The sub-classical conclusion is independent of these regions: leave-one-out shifts the weighted mean by $<6\%$, and dropping Aquila leaves the region-Plummer median at $1.40$ and the fixed-width characteristic at $2.1$, both sub-classical. The primary/limited split is likewise not tuned to the distance revision: its criteria act on the distance-independent core count and skeleton morphology, the closest any region lies to the $N>500$ boundary is Taurus at $536$ cores ($\sim7\%$ above), and because the Gaia revision rescales spacings but not core counts, no plausible distance change moves a region across the classification.

\subsection{Choice of spacing statistic}
\label{sec:statistics}

We adopt the along-skeleton adjacent-core spacing (ACS), denoted $s_{\rm ACS,med}$ for its per-region median, as the primary local measure, and report the all-pairs median only for comparability with prior work. It is not the Euclidean nearest-neighbour distance, which we use only for the Clark--Evans statistic of Section~\ref{sec:clustering}.

An all-pairs median, used as a local-spacing estimator, is intrinsically dominated by the extent of the selected segment. For $N$ points distributed over a segment of length $L$ it converges to $d^\star=L(1-1/\sqrt{2})\approx0.293\,L$, the median of the triangular pair-distance law, for a random and a perfectly periodic distribution alike (Appendix~\ref{sec:pwmedian}). It tracks that value for any filament with $N\gtrsim5$ cores and never approaches the local spacing, which is $30$--$100$ times smaller. The order statistic is elementary; what matters here is its consequence for a statistic in common use.

Synthetic point processes confirm this directly. For cores placed by periodic, clustered (gamma-renewal), hierarchical (three projected fibres) and fractal processes with a fixed injected local scale, the ACS median is flat with segment extent and recovers each process's local scale, whereas the pairwise median rises as $0.29\,L$ for \emph{every} process, independent of the injected scale.

We reconstruct the along-skeleton core ordering for the four primary regions from the deposited DisPerSE skeleton maps, bridging fragmented segments by morphological closing (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}). Figure~\ref{fig:spacing} shows the per-region spacings.

\subsection{Injection--recovery validation and model dependence of the correction}
\label{sec:injection_recovery}

To test whether the ACS statistic recovers known spacings under realistic HGBS conditions, we placed synthetic cores with known periodic spacings (0.15--0.35\,pc) on HGBS-property-matched DisPerSE skeletons for all 8 regions. On clean skeletons the raw measurement recovers the input to about $3$\%; the larger correction actually applied is the \emph{full-coverage pipeline} bias ($\div1.11$--$1.29$, from junction/branch topology on the real bridged skeletons), which moves raw $2.1\to1.9$ and is a genuine, region-measured correction. We do \emph{not} additionally apply a point-process model factor (periodic $\div1.20$ vs clustered $\div1.64$): if the cores are clustered the measured ACS median already \emph{is} the physical line-density quantity, so correcting it toward a periodic template the data reject would be circular. We therefore carry the raw value as the adopted $(s_{\rm ACS,med}/W)_0$ and quote the clustered branch ($\div1.64$: raw $2.0\to1.2$) only as a lower bound. The sub-classical \emph{sign} holds under every choice.

Repeating the exercise for the pairwise median confirms it does not recover $\lambda_{\rm true}$ (it returns a value $\sim$$20\times$ larger, tracking $L/3$), whereas the ACS statistic recovers $\lambda_{\rm true}$ to $1.8$--$3\%$ on the identical skeletons (Appendix~\ref{sec:pwmedian}).

\textbf{Full-coverage along-skeleton ACS.} We extend the ACS measurement to all four primary regions using the dense deposited DisPerSE skeletons, which (unlike the sparse FilFinder spines) pass near essentially all cores. Bridging the fragmented skeletons by morphological closing plus 1-pixel re-skeletonization, ordering cores by graph arclength and associating within a half-width ($\sim$0.06\,pc), we obtain $N_{\rm pairs}=461,614,563,168$ spacings (Taurus, Orion~B, Perseus, Aquila), median ACS $0.124,0.207,0.263,0.210$\,pc, i.e.\ $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W=1.2,2.1,2.6,2.1$ (characteristic $\approx2.1$; robust to the bridging/association radii). The periodic pipeline factor gives $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W\approx1.0,1.9,2.1,1.8$ (characteristic $\approx1.9$). Applied to every width convention (Section~\ref{sec:widths}), the fixed-width characteristic is $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W\approx1.2$--$1.9$ and the region-Plummer $\approx0.8$--$1.4$; the primary observed result is this DisPerSE envelope $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W\simeq0.8$--$1.9$ (wider under other filament definitions; Fig.~\ref{fig:envelope}), within which the raw region-Plummer $(s_{\rm ACS,med}/W)_0\approx1.4$ is a reference convention rather than the observed value carried with the correction as a uniform systematic. \textbf{The full-coverage ACS spacing ($s_{\rm ACS,med}/W\approx1.2$--$2.1$), which is conditional on being inside a core-bearing stretch, is sub-classical by a factor $\approx2.4$--$4.3$ across the DisPerSE conventions ($\approx3.6$--$4.3$ for the region-Plummer reference alone; more under FilFinder), while the pairwise-median ratio $\approx2.8$ (in $W$ units) overestimates it by $\sim$45--50\% through the $L/3$ bias.} Taurus is the most sub-classical region, not an outlier. The two high-coverage clouds (Perseus, Taurus; $76/89\%$ core--skeleton association) alone give the same median ($s_{\rm ACS,med}/W=1.8$ and $1.1$, median $1.4$), so the result is not driven by the low-coverage clouds.

This is a segmentation sensitivity test rather than an independent confirmation: it uses the same \textit{Herschel} maps and source catalogue, only an independent way of defining the filament network, running the same along-skeleton BFS-arclength ACS pipeline on independently extracted FilFinder spine maps (all four primary regions) gives $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W\approx0.4/0.6/0.7/0.8$ for Taurus, Orion~B, Perseus, Aquila respectively, systematically $2$--$3\times$ smaller than the DisPerSE values ($1.0/1.9/2.1/1.8$) because the FilFinder mask threshold produces a denser, more branched network. Both methods agree the characteristic spacing is far sub-classical ($s_{\rm ACS,med}/W\ll4$) in every region; FilFinder if anything strengthens the conclusion. The injection--recovery estimator test (Section~\ref{sec:injection_recovery_model}) was run on the DisPerSE skeletons; for FilFinder, its denser, more-branched topology (Fig.~\ref{fig:skeleton_compare}) is precisely why it returns shorter spacings, so we carry the FilFinder value only as the extreme of the convention envelope, not as a load-bearing number; a matched FilFinder injection--recovery, reported in the next paragraph, confirms the shorter values are genuine rather than an estimator artefact.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Model dependence of the correction.}\label{sec:injection_recovery_model}
Deriving for each region a correction matched to its own measured clustering (anchored to the periodic $\div1.20$ and Poisson $\div1.64$ endpoints) gives region-matched factors $\div1.40$--$1.86$ and corrected $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W\approx0.7$--$1.6$ (fixed $W=0.10$\,pc). The effect on the characteristic is small (mean $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W$ $1.2\to1.3$): the sub-classical result holds under each region's own matched correction, and $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W\approx1.9$ (which requires the rejected periodic model) remains excluded.

As a matched, independent-segmentation test, we ran the same synthetic injection--recovery on the deposited FilFinder spines: cores placed at known arclength spacings ($0.15$--$0.35$\,pc) are recovered to $<1\%$ (mean $|{\rm err}|=0.2$--$1.0\%$ across the four clouds). The shorter FilFinder $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W$ is therefore \emph{not} an estimator or branching artefact; it reflects that FilFinder traces a denser, more-branched network; a genuinely different definition of ``the filament'' (Section~\ref{sec:whatfilament}), not a measurement bias.

\subsection{Core line density, the local-width test, and the coverage bias}
\label{sec:linedensity}
The median adjacent spacing measures the spacing \emph{where cores are adjacent}; the quantity a one-fragment-per-wavelength theory actually predicts is the core \emph{line density}, cores per unit filament length. We therefore report three complementary statistics, all normalised by the \emph{same} fixed $0.10$\,pc width so that they are directly comparable. (i)~The median adjacent spacing is $S_{\rm local}\approx1.7$--$3.0$, sub-classical in every cloud. (ii)~The mean adjacent spacing is a factor $1.3$--$2.2$ larger ($\approx2.1$--$4.7$), but on the heavily-bridged skeletons it is inflated by arclength-ordering jumps across branches (the same effect that lifts Perseus's ACS above its pairwise median; Section~\ref{sec:statistics}), so we treat it as an upper descriptor, not the line density. (iii)~The direct core line density is $\rho_{\rm core,1D}=N/L$, the associated cores divided by the total skeleton arclength. For comparison with a wavelength we quote its reciprocal $L/N$, the mean filament length per associated core; this is ordering-independent and is the correct quantity to set against a one-fragment-per-wavelength prediction: $L/N=0.34$--$0.58$\,pc, i.e.\ $(L/N)/W\approx3.4$ (Orion~B), $3.4$ (Aquila), $4.5$ (Taurus), $5.8$ (Perseus). It is a factor $\approx2$ larger than the median adjacent spacing in every cloud. On the global length per core the deficit is therefore much milder: Perseus is essentially classical, Taurus near-classical, and only Orion~B and Aquila sub-classical at $\approx3.4$. Two contributions produce this near-classical value, and neither is small-scale clustering. The median$\to$mean rise ($\approx40$--$120\%$) is the \emph{generic mean/median skew} of a broad gap distribution (a pure exponential already gives $1.44$; Section~\ref{sec:clustering}), not clustering; the further mean$\to L/N$ rise is \emph{dilution by core-poor filament}; extensive skeleton stretches with no cores (a coverage effect). The line density restricted to core-bearing stretches (the mean adjacent spacing, $\approx2.1$--$4.7\,W$) is intermediate and still mildly sub-classical, so the globally near-classical $L/N$ is \emph{not} evidence of a uniform classical spacing: it is a spatially inhomogeneous distribution with sub-classical local spacing and an observational small-separation deficit, whose filament-averaged density is lifted toward classical by the skew and by long core-free filament. The median adjacent ratio adopted elsewhere is accordingly a \emph{descriptor} of the local spacing within core-bearing stretches; $L/N$ (core-bearing or full) is the quantity to compare with a one-fragment-per-$\lambda_{\rm m}$ theory.

Two further tests support this reading. First, a \emph{local-width} normalisation: for every adjacent-core interval we fit the beam-deconvolved transverse width at the interval midpoint and form the per-cloud distribution of $\Delta s/W_{\rm local}$ ($146$--$545$ reliable fits per cloud). The medians are $0.61,0.91,1.05,0.99$ (Taurus, Orion~B, Perseus, Aquila), \emph{all} sub-classical, with only a weak spacing--width correlation ($r=-0.06$ to $0.31$). These median-of-ratios values are if anything \emph{more} sub-classical than the corresponding ratio-of-medians ($\approx1.4$--$1.9$ in the skewed clouds), differing by up to a factor $\sim2$ in Taurus and Perseus; the per-segment normalisation therefore strengthens the sub-classical conclusion. Second, the along-skeleton \emph{pair-correlation} function $g(s)$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:paircorr}) shows \emph{no} peak at the classical scale $5$--$6\,W$ ($\approx0.5$--$0.8$\,pc): $g\approx1$ there (slightly below unity in Orion~B and Aquila), with a mild small-separation excess in Taurus and weak region-envelope features near $1$\,pc. To decide whether the absence of a feature is significant rather than merely visual, we generated a $95$ per cent envelope for $g(s)$ from the same column-density-conditioned null (specified in full in Appendix~\ref{sec:nullspec}). At the classical scale the observed $g$ lies \emph{inside} that envelope in every cloud (Orion~B $1.02$ within $[0.84,1.26]$; Aquila $0.89$ within $[0.58,1.52]$; Perseus $1.02$ within $[0.91,1.14]$; Taurus $1.00$ within $[0.91,1.10]$), so no classical-scale periodicity is detected at the $95$ per cent level.

\subsection{An exploratory diagnostic: the column-density-selected eligible length}
\label{sec:eligible}

Identifying $L/N$ with the quantity a one-fragment-per-wavelength theory predicts assumes that the whole skeleton could have fragmented. Much of it could not: a subcritical stretch has no axial instability to express. The estimate below is exploratory: the criterion is a column-density proxy for a line mass, and it is partly determined by the cores it is used to interpret. None of the conclusions of this paper rests on it.

We define eligibility \emph{independently of where the cores are}, using column density. A filament of width $0.10$\,pc reaches the critical line mass $M_{\rm line,crit}=2c_s^2/G\simeq16\,M_\odot\,{\rm pc^{-1}}$ at $N_{\rm H_2}\simeq7.2\times10^{21}\,{\rm cm^{-2}}$ ($\mu=2.8$), and we take the \emph{column-density-selected} eligible length $L_{\rm elig}$ to be the skeleton length above that threshold. This is a proxy, not a line-mass measurement: the conversion depends on width, inclination, temperature, background subtraction and both non-thermal and magnetic support. The eligible fractions differ greatly between clouds, $0.19$, $0.91$, $0.13$ and $0.07$ for Orion~B, Aquila, Perseus and Taurus, so Orion~B in particular is dominated by filament that could not have fragmented at all.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{The criterion is partly endogenous.} A core raises the column density of its own surroundings, so a threshold applied to the raw map can be crossed \emph{by the core it is meant to be independent of}, selecting short eligible patches centred on cores. Between $25$ and $34$ per cent of skeleton pixels lie within one association half-width of a core, so this is not a small effect. We therefore rebuilt the criterion on a core-masked background: pixels within a masking radius of an associated core have their column density replaced by interpolation along the spine from untainted pixels, and the threshold is applied to that reconstruction.

The correction is substantial. On the core-masked background $S_{\rm elig}$ becomes $2.0$, $3.2$, $4.1$ and $3.1$, against the raw $1.6$, $3.2$, $2.0$ and $0.8$. Aquila is unchanged because almost all of its skeleton is eligible either way, but Perseus doubles and Taurus rises by nearly a factor of four, and $N_{\rm elig}$ falls from $199$ to $57$ in Perseus and from $186$ to $13$ in Taurus.

The statistic is sensitive to the masking radius, and only two clouds are stable against it (Table~\ref{tab:maskrad}). Orion~B ($1.89$--$2.04$) and Aquila ($3.14$--$3.21$) are stable and retain more than $230$ cores. Perseus drifts over $3.6$--$4.5$ while $N_{\rm elig}$ falls from $85$ to $37$. In Taurus the statistic does not stabilise at all: it takes values $1.8$, $1.6$ and $3.1$ at masking radii of $0.03$, $0.045$ and $0.06$\,pc, and beyond $0.09$\,pc a single core survives, so no eligible line density can be defined there. A non-parametric bootstrap over the surviving Perseus cores gives $S_{\rm elig}=4.1^{+1.1}_{-0.9}$ ($68$ per cent), so even where the sample is nominally usable the uncertainty is large.

Sensitivity to the threshold itself runs the other way and is mild: raising it from $4\times10^{21}$ to $1.2\times10^{22}\,{\rm cm^{-2}}$ on the raw map moves $S_{\rm elig}$ monotonically \emph{downwards}, so our fiducial choice is the conservative one, and using each region's own Plummer width or $T=15$\,K changes it by less than $20$ per cent. Replacing the thermal line mass by a virial one, $M_{\rm line,vir}=2(c_s^2+\sigma_{\rm nt}^2)/G$, lowers $S_{\rm elig}$ further, but treating $\sigma_{\rm nt}$ as isotropic pressure support is a prescription rather than a measurement, and beyond the transonic case the statistic degenerates entirely. We have no per-region velocity dispersions and treat this as an illustrative band.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{What distinguishes a fertile stretch from a sterile one?} Supercriticality is necessary but not sufficient: in Aquila $90$ per cent of the crest is eligible yet only $24$ per cent carries a core. Local compression, longitudinal velocity gradients of $\sim1$\,km\,s$^{-1}$\,pc$^{-1}$ \citep{Kirk2013,Hacar2013}, and spatially varying non-thermal support are all plausible controls, and none can be tested with column density alone. Deciding among them requires resolved kinematics per stretch, so the occupancy is reported here as a measured property whose cause is undetermined.

The picture that emerges is intermediate. Part of the near-classical $S_{\rm global}$ does reflect filament that was never eligible to fragment, and part of the apparently short eligible spacing reflects the cores' own contribution to the selecting column density. With the endogeneity removed, $S_{\rm elig}$ lies between $S_{\rm local}$ and $S_{\rm global}$ in the two clouds where it can be measured. Resolved kinematics per stretch would be needed to convert this diagnostic into a measurement.

\subsection{Robustness to the persistence threshold}
\label{sec:persistence}

The DisPerSE skeletons we use are the published HGBS products, extracted at a $3\sigma$ persistence cut. Since Section~\ref{sec:error_budget} identifies the skeleton algorithm as the largest single systematic, the persistence threshold of our primary ontology cannot be left untested. We cannot regenerate the published skeletons at a \emph{lower} threshold without the original DisPerSE run, so we approach the question from both sides.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{(i) Pruning the published skeleton upwards.} We decompose each skeleton into branches, terminating at junction pixels, and compute for each branch the persistence of its column-density maximum above the junction at which it attaches, in units of the local map noise $\sigma$ (estimated as $1.4826\times$MAD of the map minus a $5$-pixel median filter). Branches below a chosen threshold are removed and the procedure iterated to convergence. Raising the effective threshold from the published $3\sigma$ to $4\sigma$ removes $16$, $21$, $18$ and $15$ per cent of the skeleton length in Orion~B, Aquila, Perseus and Taurus, but changes the median adjacent spacing by only $-8.2$, $-2.7$, $+0.3$ and $-5.0$ per cent and the length per core by $-1.9$, $-3.2$, $-3.1$ and $-3.7$ per cent. Going further, to $6\sigma$ and $8\sigma$, changes nothing further at the per-cent level: the pruning saturates once the low-persistence spurs are gone. The occupancy fraction rises slightly, as expected when bare low-persistence branches are removed.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{(ii) An extraction with a tunable threshold.} Reaching the $2\sigma$ direction requires a crest extraction whose persistence threshold is a free parameter. We construct one directly from the column-density field: a descending-sweep merge tree pairs every maximum with the saddle at which it merges, maxima whose persistence falls below the chosen multiple of $\sigma$ are cancelled, and the surviving crests are traced by steepest-ascent walks from the retained saddles. This is not DisPerSE and does not reproduce the published skeleton pixel for pixel; it is used only to vary the threshold. Run at $2\sigma$, $3\sigma$ and $4\sigma$ on Orion~B it gives $S_{\rm local}=1.63$, $1.63$ and $1.63$ and $S_{\rm global}=2.53$, $2.50$ and $2.48$, with the associated core count falling from $635$ to $597$ and the occupancy fraction rising from $0.41$ to $0.42$. The local statistic is thus insensitive to the persistence threshold at the third significant figure over a factor of two in threshold, and the global statistic moves by two per cent.

\smallskip\noindent Both tests agree: the persistence threshold is not what makes the DisPerSE and FilFinder answers differ. Between $2\sigma$ and $8\sigma$ the local spacing moves by less than ten per cent, whereas changing the ontology from persistent crests to the medial axis of a mask moves it by a factor of two. The dominant systematic is therefore the \emph{definition} of a filament, not the tuning of a parameter within one definition, which is the conclusion of Section~\ref{sec:whatfilament}. The manual editing applied to the published skeletons cannot be varied in this way and remains an unquantified term.

\subsection{Does the local/global contrast survive changing the definition of a filament?}
\label{sec:ontology}

A skeleton defines $L$ itself, so changing the extraction algorithm alters the spacing, the length, the core association and the topology together. DisPerSE and FilFinder are not two noisy estimates of one observable but partly different definitions of the object, and we therefore recomputed \emph{all} the principal statistics under both (Table~\ref{tab:ontology}).

The FilFinder networks are shorter ($67$--$121$\,pc against $106$--$337$\,pc) and associate far fewer catalogue cores, so both statistics move, but they move in \emph{opposite} directions: $S_{\rm local}$ becomes shorter still ($0.54$--$1.54$ against $1.66$--$2.97$) while $S_{\rm global}$ becomes much larger ($9.1$--$16.7$ against $3.4$--$5.8$). The local/global contrast therefore survives the change of ontology and in fact widens, from a factor $\approx2$ to $\approx6$--$17$, and the occupancy fraction stays low under both. We regard this as one of the more secure results here, precisely because the two algorithms disagree so strongly about what a filament is.

At a high-column hub, FilFinder recovers the main ridge but adds long, almost straight spokes that leave the emission entirely (Fig.~\ref{fig:skeleton_compare}). These are most likely medial-axis artefacts of a large connected mask rather than resolved sub-filaments, though we have not quantified what fraction of the total length behaves this way and column density alone cannot decide: position--position--velocity data are needed to establish which network corresponds to a coherent physical object. We therefore base our quantitative conclusions on the persistent-crest network and carry FilFinder as an ontology test.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Coverage bias.} Between $41$ and $86$ per cent of catalogue cores associate with the skeleton (Table~\ref{tab:nnpipeline}), and the rejected cores have projected separations $1.5$--$3.0$ times \emph{larger}, so coverage selection biases the adjacent spacing short. Relaxing the association half-width from $0.06$ to $0.30$\,pc raises coverage to $70$--$97$ per cent and lengthens the median adjacent spacing by only $3$--$16$ per cent (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaletests}c); $S_{\rm local}$ plateaus and the bias is bounded at $\lesssim16$ per cent.

The unconditional statistic behaves differently, and this is a genuine limitation. Because adding cores at fixed $L$ necessarily raises the line density, $S_{\rm global}$ falls monotonically with association radius, from $3.6$ to $2.1$ in Orion~B, $3.6$ to $2.0$ in Aquila, $6.6$ to $4.2$ in Perseus and $5.1$ to $4.0$ in Taurus. The near-classical value is robust in Perseus and Taurus but in Orion~B and Aquila holds only near the adopted radius. The local/global contrast persists in all four clouds; its size in the two low-coverage clouds depends on how generously cores are assigned.

\subsection{Spatial inhomogeneity: filament occupancy and scale-dependent over-dispersion}
\label{sec:intermittency}

The gap between $S_{\rm local}$ and $S_{\rm global}$ implies that cores occupy preferential stretches. This can be measured. We assign each associated core an occupancy interval of $\pm0.06$\,pc along its spine, merge overlapping intervals, and define the occupancy fraction $f_{\rm occ}$ as the fraction of skeleton length that is core-bearing; we also count cores in $1$\,pc cells and form the Fano factor $F=\sigma^2/\mu$ and the Gini coefficient of those counts (Table~\ref{tab:intermittency}, Fig.~\ref{fig:intermittency}).

Between $6$ and $27$ per cent of the skeleton carries cores, so most filament length is empty at HGBS sensitivity. The Fano factor exceeds unity everywhere ($1.6$, $2.8$, $5.7$, $25.2$ for Aquila, Orion~B, Perseus and Taurus) and the Gini coefficients are $0.36$--$0.57$. In Orion~B, $92$\,pc of the $337$\,pc skeleton is occupied, a further $155$\,pc lies in core-free runs inside core-bearing components, and roughly $90$\,pc is in components with no catalogued core at all.

Because $f_{\rm occ}$ necessarily grows with the assumed interval, we report it against $\ell$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaletests}a). Taurus rises only from $0.052$ to $0.078$ and Perseus from $0.078$ to $0.202$ as $\ell$ increases tenfold, so widening the interval adds almost nothing; Orion~B and Aquila climb to $\approx0.6$ and are much less segregated. The Fano factor is the more robust statistic because it needs no occupancy convention: $F(\ell)>1$ across $\ell=0.25$--$4$\,pc in Taurus ($9.4$--$25$), Perseus ($2.3$--$8.4$) and Orion~B ($1.2$--$4.9$), rising monotonically with $\ell$. Aquila is the exception, consistent with Poisson below $1$\,pc.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Is this more than the filament's own structure implies?} A Fano factor above unity compares the cores with a \emph{homogeneous} Poisson process, and a filament is not homogeneous. We therefore built an inhomogeneous null whose intensity is conditioned on the local column density, measured $\lambda(N_{\rm H_2})$ from the data, scattered synthetic cores along the actual skeletons and passed them through the same pipeline, in $10^4$ realisations per cloud (Appendix~\ref{sec:nullspec}). In Orion~B, Aquila and Perseus the observed $F$ lies \emph{below} the null median ($p=0.98$, $0.88$, $0.96$): the column-density structure alone accounts for all of the over-dispersion. Only Taurus exceeds its null ($F=25.2$ against a median of $13.4$; $p=2\times10^{-4}$). Refitting the intensity out of sample, from the other three clouds, leaves this unchanged.

One bias remains and it works against us: cores raise the column density of their own surroundings, so the pixels to which the null assigns the highest intensity are the core sites themselves, and the null is too easily satisfied. Masking a $0.06$\,pc radius around each associated core changes the intensity in the highest column bins by factors of $4$--$10$. The three non-detections are therefore conservative rather than secure, and the Taurus excess is correspondingly strengthened. Excess clumping is therefore detected in one of the four clouds and not detected in the other three, which is not the same as its being absent there.

Two length scales coexist. On sub-parsec scales the gap distribution shows a deficit of the smallest separations (Section~\ref{sec:clustering}); on parsec scales the counts are strongly over-dispersed. Cores form in groups with short internal separations, and the groups are separated by long stretches that have produced none. This accounts for the factor of about two between $S_{\rm local}$ and $S_{\rm global}$ without appealing to small-scale clustering, and it is the central observational result of this paper: what a fragmentation model must reproduce is a spatial point process, not a single spacing.

\subsection{Regularity of the core spacing}
\label{sec:clustering}

Classical fragmentation predicts a quasi-periodic chain; fibre projection or a turbulent cascade predicts a broad distribution with no comb. Ordering associated cores by arclength and measuring adjacent gaps gives a coefficient of variation of order unity (Taurus $1.36$, Orion~B $0.83$, Perseus $0.96$, Aquila $0.65$; Fig.~\ref{fig:clustering}).

Goodness-of-fit tests were calibrated by parametric bootstrap ($N_{\rm boot}=10^5$), resampling from the fitted distribution to build the exact null distribution of the Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic. The result is two-sided: the regular ``periodic$+$jitter'' model is rejected ($p_{\rm boot}<10^{-5}$ in every cloud) \emph{and} so is the pure exponential ($p_{\rm boot}<10^{-5}$ in three clouds, $1.7\times10^{-3}$ in Perseus). Comparing five renewal models by the corrected Akaike criterion makes this quantitative: $\Delta$AICc$=37$--$246$ against Poisson and $81$--$742$ against the periodic comb in every cloud. The specific hard-core (shifted-exponential) model is not uniquely preferred once broader alternatives are admitted, a positively-skewed lognormal fitting best in three clouds, so the data favour a broad, skewed distribution truncated at small separations rather than a sharp physical hard core.

The clouds fail the exponential for different reasons and should not be summarised together (Table~\ref{tab:percloud}). Writing $R$ for the observed median gap divided by that expected for an exponential of the same mean, only Orion~B ($1.03$) and Aquila ($1.14$) show a deficit of the smallest separations; Perseus ($0.92$) and Taurus ($0.64$) show an excess.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{The small-separation deficit is not shown to be physical.} The inferred exclusion scale, $0.011$--$0.044$\,pc, lies entirely within the instrumental floor: the $18.2''$ beam corresponds to $0.012$--$0.039$\,pc across our distance range and \textsc{getsources} does not reliably separate sources closer than about one beam. We can place no lower limit on a physical contribution, and any thermal Jeans-scale suppression would act at $0.05$--$0.1$\,pc, above the measured scale. Separating the two requires synthetic source pairs injected into the maps and re-extracted through the HGBS pipeline; until that is done we treat the deficit as observational.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Gap independence.} A runs test about the median gap shows significant non-independence in Perseus ($z=-4.2$) and Taurus ($z=-5.4$), the same core-poor/core-rich alternation documented in Section~\ref{sec:intermittency}. We therefore recomputed $\Delta$AICc with a moving-block bootstrap that resamples contiguous runs of five adjacent gaps drawn from within single components ($400$ resamples per cloud). The effective sample size falls to $122$ of $440$ gaps in Taurus and $344$ of $438$ in Perseus, but the rejections survive: the $5$th percentile of the bootstrap $\Delta$AICc is $415$, $45$, $249$ and $587$ against the periodic model and $190$, $71$, $31$ and $55$ against Poisson. What does not survive is discrimination among the broad models, the shifted-exponential lying inside the lognormal's bootstrap interval in three clouds.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{No classical-scale periodicity is detected.} The along-skeleton pair-correlation $g(s)$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:paircorr}) shows no feature at $5$--$6\,W$. To decide whether that absence is significant we generated a $95$ per cent envelope from the column-density-conditioned null of Appendix~\ref{sec:nullspec}: at the classical scale the observed $g$ lies \emph{inside} the envelope in every cloud (Orion~B $1.02$ in $[0.84,1.26]$; Aquila $0.89$ in $[0.58,1.52]$; Perseus $1.02$ in $[0.91,1.14]$; Taurus $1.00$ in $[0.91,1.10]$). This is a non-detection at the $95$ per cent level, not a demonstration of absence, and it is more informative than a comparison of median spacings because it does not compress the point process into a single number.

\subsection{Systematic error budget}
\label{sec:error_budget}

Table~\ref{tab:errorbudget} collects the terms. We separate \emph{statistical/sensitivity} terms, which would change under resampling, from \emph{systematic} offsets and from \emph{modelling} terms arising in the simulations. Uncorrelated statistical terms are combined in quadrature, $\sigma_{\rm sys}^2=\sum_i\sigma_i^2$; the multiplicative width- and skeleton-convention factors are applied afterwards as separate analysis-choice envelopes rather than added into that sum. The adopted $(s_{\rm ACS,med}/W)_0\approx1.4$ carries residual pipeline bias ($\sim10$ per cent), protostellar migration ($\sim7.5$ per cent), between-region scatter ($\sim4$ per cent) and a $\lesssim5$ per cent manual-editing proxy in quadrature, with the width convention ($\sim25$ per cent) and the skeleton algorithm (a factor $\sim2$) applied on top. Because the dominant term is a deterministic offset we quote a min--max envelope, $S_{\rm local}\approx0.8$--$1.9$ for DisPerSE, rather than a Gaussian $\sigma$.

\emph{Projection} acts against our conclusion and is shown in Table~\ref{tab:headline} for that reason. For an isotropic orientation distribution $s_{\rm obs}/s_{\rm true}=\sin i$ has median $0.87$, so deprojection raises the intrinsic spacing by $15$--$27$ per cent, moving the adopted $1.4$ to $1.6$--$1.8$. We do not apply it, because the orientation distribution within a single cloud need not be isotropic, but we carry it as a one-sided systematic.

\emph{Skeleton-algorithm dependence} is the largest observational term. The DisPerSE and FilFinder values ($\approx1.9$ and $\approx0.4$--$0.8$) bracket a genuine ambiguity in what constitutes ``the filament'' rather than a measurement error, and a matched-threshold FilFinder extraction shows the inferred spacing is itself strongly sensitive to the mask threshold, so the factor of two is if anything a lower bound. We base our quantitative conclusions on the persistent-crest network, which is the physically better-motivated definition of a filament ridge, and treat FilFinder as an ontology test rather than an equally weighted measurement.

\subsection{Comparison with the literature}

Our revised-distance ACS measurements (Table~\ref{tab:literature}) differ from the original HGBS values by the region-dependent distance revisions; the core-count-weighted primary-region mean rises only $\sim10$ per cent, dominated by Aquila. Toggling each methodological choice on the Orion~B data at fixed distance shows that the \emph{statistic definition} dominates: switching from ACS to the pairwise median inflates the spacing from $0.207$ to $0.313$\,pc and accounts for essentially the whole apparent shift, while skeleton re-extraction, association half-width, arclength ordering and the distance revision are each $\leq15$ per cent and largely cancel. On our primary statistic Orion~B agrees with \citet{Konyves2020} to within $3$ per cent.

The published values we could check are local or along-fibre separations, not all-pairs medians, which is why they agree with our ACS column rather than our pairwise column. The region-extent bias applies wherever an all-pairs or pooled statistic is adopted, including the comparability statistic carried here and in cross-region compilations, and we make no claim that it affects these particular published values.

\subsection{What constitutes the theoretical filament?}
\label{sec:whatfilament}
The factor-of-two DisPerSE-versus-FilFinder difference (Fig.~\ref{fig:skeleton_compare}) is not measurement noise but reflects that the two algorithms \emph{define different objects} (persistent crests versus the medial axis of a masked skeleton), so there is no single, algorithm-independent ``filament'' whose spacing theory should reproduce. Our robustness claim applies to the \emph{sign} of the deficit, every convention gives a sub-classical $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W$, while its \emph{magnitude} cannot be assigned a unique value: the same data yield $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W\approx1.0$ (region-Plummer FilFinder), $\approx1.4$ (region-Plummer DisPerSE) or $\approx1.9$ (fixed-width DisPerSE). Because segmentation alone moves the answer by as much as the differences between the physical models compared in Section~\ref{sec:validation_endtoend}, the theory should not be read as discriminating between these values. \section{Theoretical framework}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Classical fragmentation theory}




For an isothermal, self-gravitating cylinder of density $\rho_0$ and sound speed $c_s$, the critical line mass for gravitational instability is \citep{Ostriker1964}:
\begin{equation}
\mu_{\rm crit} = \frac{2c_s^2}{G}
\label{eq:mucrit}
\end{equation}
The most unstable fragmentation wavelength is, as an \emph{exact} result for the isothermal equilibrium cylinder \citep{Inutsuka1992},
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\rm IM92} = 22\,H = 4\times(2R_{\rm flat}),
\label{eq:im92}
\end{equation}
where $H = c_s\,(4\pi G\rho_c)^{-1/2}$ is the thermal scale height, $R_{\rm flat}=\sqrt8\,H$ the flattening radius, and $W_{\rm fil}\approx0.1$\,pc the characteristic filament FWHM. Solving the dispersion relation exactly (Appendix~\ref{sec:validation}; \citealt{Fischera2012}) gives a fastest-growing wavelength $\lambda_{\rm m}\approx5\times W_{\rm fil}$ near-critical, rising to $\approx6\times W_{\rm fil}$ at low line mass, and a critical wavelength $\lambda_{\rm cr}\approx2.6\times W_{\rm fil}$. The often-quoted ``$\lambda\approx4\times$ the width'' is $4\times$ the flat \emph{diameter} $2R_{\rm flat}$ \citep{Inutsuka1992}, which equals $\approx5$--$6\times$ the \emph{FWHM}; taking it as $4\times$ the FWHM understates the classical scale. We therefore adopt $\lambda_{\rm m}\approx5$--$6\,W_{\rm fil}$ (Fischera \& Martin 2012) as the benchmark. Because we compare in FWHM units and forward-model the simulations to a beam-convolved Plummer FWHM (Section~\ref{sec:width_correction}), the sub-classical deficit does not depend on the equilibrium-vs-observed profile-index difference ($p=4$ vs $p\simeq2$), and we compare as the density-independent ratio $\lambda/W_{\rm fil}$. The deficit is not new in sign: \citet{Fischera2012} noted observed cores sit ``about the FWHM'' apart, and \citet{Zhang2023cal} find a spacing $\approx1.3\times$ the width in California.

Two things follow. The ratio $\lambda_{\rm m}/H=22$ and the conversion $\lambda_{\rm m}\approx4\times(2R_{\rm flat})$ are properties of the $p=4$ equilibrium and are exact within it. What is \emph{not} invariant is $\lambda_{\rm m}/W_{\rm FWHM}$, because the FWHM of a $p\simeq2$ Plummer profile is a different fraction of $H$ from that of a $p=4$ profile, and beam convolution broadens it further. The observational benchmark is therefore convention-dependent at the tens-of-per-cent level even before any measurement error, which is one reason we treat the simulated comparison through forward modelling (Section~\ref{sec:width_correction}) rather than through this ratio.

With $f=\mu_{\rm line}/\mu_{\rm crit}$ and $t_{\rm frag}\propto(f-1)^{-1/2}$, marginally supercritical filaments fragment slowly. At magnetically supercritical field strength ($\beta\gtrsim0.2$) all configurations fragment for $\mathcal{M}\geq1$ at every $f$ tested once integrated adequately: above the critical line mass there is no \emph{thermal} stability boundary. This is distinct from \emph{magnetic} stabilisation, where a strong longitudinal field ($\beta\lesssim0.15$) suppresses collapse even for $f>1$ (the ``strong-field, magnetically supported'' regime; the deposited material); the thermal ($f$) and magnetic (mass-to-flux) criticalities are physically distinct.

We adopt a single reference $W_{\rm fil}=0.10$\,pc for comparability with prior HGBS work, though its universality is debated \citep{Panopoulou2017,Panopoulou2022}; the directly-measured region-Plummer widths ($0.11$--$0.16$\,pc, $\lambda/W\approx1.4$; Section~\ref{sec:widths}) show the sub-classical conclusion is robust to the convention, carried as the $\sim25\%$ width systematic (Section~\ref{sec:error_budget}).

\subsection{Width normalisation: $W_{\rm core}$ to $W_{\rm fil}$}
\label{sec:width_correction}

Three widths enter the simulation--observation comparison and must be kept separate (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic}; Table~\ref{tab:widthdefs}): the initial Gaussian half-width $W_{\rm core}=0.3\,\lambda_{\rm J}$; the formation FWHM $W_{\rm form}=0.683\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ of the evolved projected profile ($\simeq2.28\,W_{\rm core}$, slightly below the initial $2.355\,W_{\rm core}$ owing to contraction; resolution- and seed-converged to $<1\%$); and the observed HGBS width $W_{\rm fil}$, the beam-convolved Plummer FWHM \citep{Arzoumanian2019}, which we obtain by forward-modelling the simulated filaments through the full synthetic-HGBS pipeline (projection, beam convolution, Plummer fit). This gives $\eta_W=W_{\rm form}/W_{\rm fil}=0.59$--$0.78$ and
\begin{equation}
T_1 \equiv \frac{W_{\rm form}}{W_{\rm fil}} = 0.61 \quad (\text{forward-model band } 0.59\text{--}0.78),
\label{eq:t1_correction}
\end{equation}
so that $W_{\rm fil}=W_{\rm form}/T_1\approx1.12\,\lambda_{\rm J}$. We quote the forward-model best-fit $T_1=0.61$; because it lies near the lower edge of the band, a mid-band $T_1\approx0.68$ would give a slightly \emph{larger} (less sub-classical) $\lambda/W_{\rm fil}$, so this normalisation is deliberately not the headline; the primary comparison uses the width-convention-independent growth-rate upper bound (Section~\ref{sec:validation_endtoend}), and this $T_1$ conversion is retained only as a cross-check. The observable is $\lambda/W_{\rm fil}$; because the fragmentation spacing $\lambda$ is a physical length independent of the width definition, the conversion from the simulation-internal ratio $\lambda/W_{\rm core}$ (normalised by the initial $\sigma=0.3\,\lambda_{\rm J}$) is
\begin{equation}
\Big(\tfrac{\lambda}{W}\Big)_{\rm fil} = \frac{W_{\rm core}}{W_{\rm fil}}\Big(\tfrac{\lambda}{W}\Big)_{\rm core} \approx 0.27\,\Big(\tfrac{\lambda}{W}\Big)_{\rm core},
\label{eq:t1_apply}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ $\lambda/W_{\rm fil}=\lambda\,[\lambda_{\rm J}]/1.12$; the prefactor is $0.27=W_{\rm core}/W_{\rm fil}=0.30/1.12$ (equivalently $T_1$ times the $\sigma\!\to\!$FWHM factor, $0.61\times0.44=0.27$, using $T_1\,W_{\rm core}/W_{\rm form}$), and using $T_1$ alone would overstate $\lambda/W_{\rm fil}$ by $\approx2.3\times$. This prefactor is \emph{not} a fixed constant: it carries the full $\approx30\%$ $T_1$ band ($T_1=0.59$--$0.78$), so $0.27$ is the best-fit value of a $\approx0.26$--$0.34$ range. Because the observable is sensitive to how $W_{\rm fil}$ is defined, the primary comparison uses the width-convention-independent growth-rate wavelength (an upper limit; Section~\ref{sec:validation_endtoend}), and this normalisation is retained only as a cross-check.

\subsection{Magnetic tension}
\label{sec:magnetic}

For a filament threaded by a magnetic field at an angle $\theta$ to the axis (Alfv\'en speed $v_A$), a schematic oblique dispersion relation, valid only for a static background field, is \citep{Nakamura1993}:
\begin{equation}
\omega^2 = c_s^2 k^2 + v_A^2 k^2 \sin^2\theta - 4\pi G\rho_0 F(kR)
\label{eq:dispersion}
\end{equation}
where $F(kR)$ encodes the cylindrical geometry and $\theta$ is the field--axis angle. This is a \emph{schematic static-field} relation that treats $\mathbf{B}$ as a fixed background and \emph{cannot be used quantitatively} for a magnetised cylindrical equilibrium; in particular it does not describe a current-carrying (helical) equilibrium. We use it only as an analytic guide, so this paper contains \emph{no quantitative model of magnetic tension}; it nonetheless fixes the \emph{sign} of each idealised case, the magnetic term $v_A^2k^2\sin^2\theta$ vanishing for a \emph{longitudinal} field (reducing to the field-free thermal relation) and being maximal for a \emph{perpendicular} one ($\theta=90^\circ$, which lengthens the axial mode). Consistent with this, our longitudinal runs keep the growth-rate wavelength at order $\lambda_{\rm J}$ across $\beta=0.3$--$1.0$ ($f=1.1$; the $\beta=1.0$ ladder gives $\lambda_{\rm turn}=1.14\,\lambda_{\rm J}$, Table~\ref{tab:production}) with no resolved $\beta$-trend, so the scale is set by the thermal Jeans length, not the field, and the shortening we measure is the dynamical/contraction shift, not magnetic tension. A genuinely shorter magnetic wavelength would require a force-balanced \emph{helical}/toroidal field, whose stability is a cylindrical magnetostatic-equilibrium problem that Eq.~(\ref{eq:dispersion}) cannot address. The full case-by-case discussion of the longitudinal, perpendicular and helical geometries, and the sense in which the simulations \emph{constrain only the specific initial field geometries simulated} rather than dispose of magnetic alternatives, is deferred to Section~\ref{sec:why_mhd_fails}. The seeded helical run tested here is an \emph{out-of-equilibrium pinch}, not a force-balanced equilibrium, and does \emph{not} rule out the helical mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:optional_tests}).

\subsection{Hierarchical fragmentation}

Filament fragmentation is hierarchical: filaments fragment into velocity-coherent fibres, and fibres into cores \citep{Hacar2013,Hacar2018,Yang2024}. Whether the fibre-to-core spacing is quasi-periodic at the classical scale is not established; the ALMA study of \citet{Yang2024} finds a clear filament--fibre--core hierarchy but only a weak quasi-periodic imprint at the core level. If HGBS filaments are bundles of fibres each fragmenting near the classical scale, measuring core spacings across the whole filament compresses the apparent spacing, in the observed direction. We test this quantitatively in Section~\ref{sec:fibre_projection}, where a \emph{toy geometrical model} of $N$-fibre bundles run through our ACS pipeline reproduces the observed $s_{\rm ACS,med}/W$; it emerges as a viable but not unique explanation (single-filament dynamical fragmentation is also consistent), and confirming it requires fibre-resolved core-spacing analysis beyond the single Orion~B region of \citet{Yang2024}.

\section{MHD simulations}
\label{sec:sims}

\noindent\emph{Status of this and the following section.} The calculations below are exploratory. They are not a survey of filament fragmentation and they do not deliver a measured fragmentation wavelength: the growth-rate spectrum of a contracting filament has no resolved turnover, so every simulated number quoted here is an \emph{upper bound} $\lambda_{\rm turn}$ on where a turnover could lie. Their role is to provide an existence proof, that fragmentation from a contracting rather than an equilibrium state is not constrained to the classical wavelength, calibrated by the equilibrium-recovery control of Section~\ref{sec:validation_endtoend}. They should be read as a counterexample to a theoretical expectation, not as a prediction to be matched number-for-number against the observations.

The full campaign comprises $2251$ Athena++ runs across 18 grids, consuming of order $10^5$ CPU-hours on Google Cloud E2 infrastructure; controlled numerical experiments, not models of individual HGBS filaments. The large majority are \emph{exploratory} runs that map outcomes and timescales; the headline \emph{wavelength} result rests instead on the load-bearing \emph{production} runs (the 25-run near-critical growth-rate campaign, Table~\ref{tab:production}, and its 8-run $128^3$--$512^3$ convergence ladder; Table~\ref{tab:production}), so the $2251$-run total is \emph{not} the statistical weight behind the wavelength. Two results emerge (Section~\ref{sec:why_mhd_fails}): the \emph{outcome} of a run (radial collapse or longitudinal beading) depends on regime and boundary conditions, so a single filament has no setup-independent fate, whereas the scale on which it fragments is bounded above at a sub-classical value ($\approx0.5$--$1\times$ the classical value; Section~\ref{sec:validation_endtoend}) for near-critical, longitudinal-field filaments (the perpendicular geometry is not numerically resolved). This section provides the evidence for both.

\subsection{Simulation method}

We performed self-gravitating simulations with Athena++ v21.0 \citep{Stone2020}, solving the standard ideal-MHD equations, continuity, momentum with the Lorentz force, ideal induction, and total energy, coupled to self-gravity through the Poisson equation $\nabla^2\phi=4\pi G\rho$.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{The production initial condition, stated in full.} The production runs use a \emph{Gaussian} filament embedded in a uniform ambient medium,
\begin{equation}
\rho(r)=\rho_{\rm bg}+\rho_{\rm amp}\exp\!\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2W_{\rm core}^{2}}\right),\qquad
\rho_{\rm amp}=\frac{f\,M_{\rm line,crit}}{2\pi W_{\rm core}^{2}},
\label{eq:ic_gauss}
\end{equation}
with $r^{2}=y^{2}+z^{2}$, $\rho_{\rm bg}=1$ in code units, $M_{\rm line,crit}=2c_s^{2}/G$ and $W_{\rm core}=0.3\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ the initial Gaussian half-width $\sigma_0$ of Table~\ref{tab:symbols}. The normalisation of $\rho_{\rm amp}$ is chosen so that $\int_0^\infty(\rho-\rho_{\rm bg})\,2\pi r\,{\rm d}r=f\,M_{\rm line,crit}$ exactly; the line mass therefore converges without an imposed truncation radius, and $f$ is imposed directly rather than inferred. There is no separate truncation: the filament merges into the uniform ambient medium, which also sets the confining pressure $P_{\rm bg}=\rho_{\rm bg}c_s^{2}$. The equilibrium-recovery control instead uses the Ostriker profile available in the same problem generator,
\begin{equation}
\rho(r)=\rho_{\rm bg}+\frac{f\rho_{c,\rm ost}}{\left[1+(r/W_{\rm core})^{2}\right]^{2}},\qquad
\rho_{c,\rm ost}=\frac{2c_s^{2}}{\pi G W_{\rm core}^{2}},
\label{eq:ic_ost}
\end{equation}
that is a Plummer profile of index $p=4$, whose line mass also converges. Neither production nor control run uses a $p=2$ profile; the observed filaments have $p\simeq2$, which is why the width conversion of Section~\ref{sec:width_correction} is needed and why it is not invariant.

The remaining initial fields are $P(r)=\rho(r)c_s^{2}$ (isothermal, $c_s=1$), $v_r(r)=0$ and $v_\phi(r)=0$, a uniform field $\mathbf{B}_0$ of magnitude $B_0=c_s\sqrt{2\rho_{\rm bg}/\beta}$ oriented at $\theta$ to the axis, and a longitudinal velocity seed built from twelve Kolmogorov modes, $v_x=\sum_{n=1}^{12}A_n\cos(k_nx+\phi_n)$ with $k_n=2\pi n/L_x$, $A_n\propto k_n^{-11/6}$ and random phases, normalised so that the total $\delta v/c_s$ equals the quoted seed amplitude. Domains are $L_x\times L_\perp^{2}$ with $L_x=16\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ for the growth-rate ladder, periodic in $x$ and, for the ambient runs, with the transverse boundary at $d\geq1\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ from the spine (a distance chosen after we found that closer transverse boundaries pin the collapse time). Every load-bearing run in Table~\ref{tab:production} uses Eq.~\ref{eq:ic_gauss}; the equilibrium control uses Eq.~\ref{eq:ic_ost} after explicit drag relaxation.

The filament is characterised by the thermal line-mass fraction $f=M_{\rm line}/M_{\rm line,crit}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:mucrit}), the plasma $\beta=8\pi P/B^2$, and the seed-amplitude parameter $M_{\rm seed}=\delta v/c_s\times10^{4}$. At filament conditions ($n\sim10^4\,{\rm cm^{-3}}$, $T\sim10$\,K) $\beta=1$--$0.3$ corresponds to $B\sim15$--$30\,\mu$G, the range of dense-filament estimates \citep{Crutcher2012}. $M_{\rm seed}$ is a perturbation-spectrum label, not a physical Mach number: the base grid uses a single-mode seed $\delta v/c_s=10^{-4}$, whereas the growth-rate ladder of Section~\ref{sec:validation_endtoend} uses a $10^{-3}$ twelve-mode broadband seed. The magnetic mass-to-flux ratio scales as $\mu_\Phi/\mu_{\Phi,\rm crit}\propto f\sqrt{\beta}$ and exceeds unity for every configuration (none strictly magnetically subcritical), so the low-$\beta$ runs are magnetically \emph{supported}.

We explored a base grid ($\mathcal{M}=0.5$--$5$, $\beta=0.05$--$5$, near-critical) and a transition grid ($f=1.4$--$2.0$, $\beta=0.3$--$1.3$, $\mathcal{M}=1$--$5$) to resolve the stable--unstable boundary; full ranges, run counts and the resulting three outcome regimes (magnetically supported quasi-stability at $\beta\lesssim0.15$, radial collapse, and longitudinal fragmentation) are deferred to Appendices~the deposited material and the deposited material. The near-critical longitudinal case that is our focus beads at $t_{\rm frag}\approx1.1$--$1.2\,t_{\rm J}$, turbulence changing the \emph{wavelength} by $<5\%$ while shortening the timescale $3$--$5\times$. The load-bearing \emph{production} runs on which the quoted wavelength rests, the near-critical growth-rate ladder and the direct supercritical ensemble, are collected in Table~\ref{tab:production}. These runs satisfy the Truelove criterion at the spacing-setting epoch ($N_J\approx5$--$7$ cells per local Jeans length, in both the axial and transverse directions on the isotropic cubic grids) but sit close to the numerical floor, only $1.25$--$1.75\times$ the $N_J=4$ minimum, which is one reason we quote only an order-$\lambda_{\rm J}$ upper bound (Appendix~\ref{sec:validation}).

\subsection{Why a contracting filament gives only an upper bound}
\label{sec:whyupper}

The numerical result rests on three physical ingredients, and because they are what makes $\lambda_{\rm turn}$ a bound rather than a measurement we set them out here rather than leaving them to the appendix.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{(i) Contraction competes with mode growth.} The classical calculation assumes a static, marginally-stable cylinder, so every unstable axial mode has unlimited time and the mode that wins is simply the one with the largest growth rate, $\lambda_{\rm m}\approx22H$. Our production filaments are not in force balance: they begin with $f\gtrsim1$ and no supporting radial velocity, so the spine contracts radially while the axial modes are still growing. The relevant comparison is between the mode growth time $\tau_{\rm grow}=\Gamma(\lambda)^{-1}$ and the time on which the background itself changes, $\tau_{\rm contract}=|{\rm d}\ln\rho_c/{\rm d}t|^{-1}$. Long-wavelength modes have the longest growth times and are the ones that fail to complete before the state they were computed for has gone; the surviving modes are those at the short end of the converged band. Global axial infall reinforces this, because material is delivered into the growing density maxima faster than an individual perturbation can detach itself, so no single mode is allowed to run away in isolation. The full argument, including why the effect should scale with the initial contraction rate, is given in Appendix~\ref{sec:nullspec}.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{(ii) That is why there is no resolved maximum.} Because the selection is set by the timescale competition rather than by the shape of $\Gamma(\lambda)$, the measured spectrum does not turn over: $\Gamma$ rises monotonically until it meets the grid scale, where the rise becomes numerical (Fig.~\ref{fig:growthrate}a). We therefore quote the converged-band edge $\lambda_{\rm turn}$, which is an \emph{upper limit} on where a physical turnover could lie, and we do not quote a peak. The equilibrium-recovery control shows that this is a property of the initial state and not of our measurement: the identical pipeline applied to a drag-relaxed Ostriker cylinder recovers the classical maximum at $22H$ to within $10$\,per cent.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{(iii) The role of the magnetic variables.} The field enters through $\beta$ and, equivalently, through the mass-to-flux ratio $\mu_\Phi/\mu_{\Phi,\rm crit}\propto f\sqrt{\beta}$, which exceeds unity for every configuration run, so none of our filaments is magnetically subcritical. For a longitudinal field the axial modes compress gas \emph{along} the field lines, so magnetic tension does no work against them and the growth-rate spectrum is close to the unmagnetised one; this is what we find, the axial spectrum being unchanged to within the fit noise between $\beta=0.3$ and $\beta=2$. The field's effect is instead radial: it resists the transverse contraction, and at $\beta\lesssim0.15$ it halts it altogether, which is the magnetically-supported quasi-stable regime of the deposited material. A field with a toroidal component would in principle act on the axial mode, but only if it is in radial force balance; a toroidal component seeded onto an already-contracting filament merely pinches it, narrowing $W$ by $\approx25$\,per cent and thereby \emph{raising} $\lambda/W$ without shortening $\lambda$. The one configuration that could genuinely shorten the axial mode, the force-balanced Fiege--Pudritz equilibrium, is not simulated here, and we are explicit in Section~\ref{sec:why_mhd_fails} that our campaign therefore does not constrain the magnetic channel.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{(iv) Bridging to observable units.} Converting a simulated $\lambda_{\rm turn}$ into something comparable with $S_{\rm local}$ or $S_{\rm global}$ requires the width to be measured the way an observer measures it. Three widths are involved (Table~\ref{tab:widthdefs}): the initial Gaussian half-width $W_{\rm core}=0.3\,\lambda_{\rm J}$, the evolved formation FWHM $W_{\rm form}=0.683\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ of the projected spine profile, and the beam-convolved Plummer FWHM $W_{\rm fil}$ that an observer fits. The bridge is a single multiplicative factor,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\lambda_{\rm turn}}{W_{\rm fil}}=\eta_W\,\frac{\lambda_{\rm turn}}{W_{\rm form}},
\qquad \eta_W\equiv\frac{W_{\rm form}}{W_{\rm fil}}=T_1,
\label{eq:etaW}
\end{equation}
with $T_1=\eta_W=0.59$--$0.78$ measured by forward-modelling the simulation output through a synthetic \textit{Herschel} beam and the same Plummer fitting used on the data (Section~\ref{sec:width_correction}). It is Eq.~\ref{eq:etaW}, and not the raw simulation number, that turns the Gaussian-normalised bound $\lambda_{\rm turn}/W_{\rm form}\approx2$ into the matched bound $S_{\rm sim}^{\rm upper}\lesssim1.0$--$1.3$ quoted throughout. The $\eta_W$ range is the dominant modelling term in Table~\ref{tab:errorbudget}, and it widens the matched bound by about a quarter.

\subsection{Ambient confinement and the direct supercritical measurement}
\label{sec:modecompetition}

The outcome for a supercritical filament is set by whether it is confined by an ambient medium, as real HGBS filaments are ($d$ denotes the axis-to-boundary distance in Jeans lengths). The main 654-run grid has no ambient medium, so its Gaussian skirt feeds an accretion channel through the periodic boundary that suppresses beading and drives radial collapse; with ambient confinement (\texttt{filament\_ambient} generator) the same configurations fragment longitudinally, a 12-run reconciliation suite beading at physical, $\beta$-dependent collapse times ($0.55$--$0.70\,t_{\rm J}$) that converge across boundary conditions.

Ambient-confined supercritical filaments ($f=1.5$--$2.0$, $\theta=0^\circ$) fragment longitudinally: an ensemble of 39 runs (periodic and reflecting BCs, ambient confinement) develops 3--10 interior axial density maxima at a spacing of order the Jeans length. We take the wavelength from the linear growth-rate spectrum, whose low-$n$ modes are resolution-stable: no physical turnover is detected above $\approx1\,\lambda_{\rm J}$, so any turnover is constrained only to $\lambda_{\rm turn}\lesssim1.0$--$1.14\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ (an upper bound; no resolved peak), $\lesssim0.35$--$0.5$ times the classical value (not the median peak count, which is epoch- and resolution-dependent). The ambient medium confines the filament (central-to-ambient density ratio $\sim10^2$, consistent with HGBS crest-to-background contrasts; \citealt{Arzoumanian2019}), so it is representative rather than tuned. Across the 39 runs periodic and reflecting boundaries are statistically indistinguishable (KS $p=0.17$; medians $\lambda/W_{\rm fil}\approx0.89$ and $1.01$), and this ensemble median and the growth-rate result are the same physical wavelength $\lambda\approx1.1\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ in different normalisations. The ensemble beads early ($t_{\rm bead}\approx0.6$--$0.75\,t_{\rm J}$), before runaway and at moderate contrast, so with $\lambda\approx\lambda_{\rm J}$ resolved by ${\sim}32$ cells it satisfies Truelove at measurement (Appendix~\ref{sec:validation}).

Perpendicular-field filaments ($\theta=90^\circ$) do not universally spindle in the raw low-resolution grids: an 18-run ideal-MHD grid at $d=1.0$ shows $7/18$ bead (at $\beta=0.5$ and $2.0$) with an apparently short spacing ($\lambda/W_{\rm fil}\approx0.4$), but this conflicts with static linear theory (which predicts a \emph{longer} mode) and a transverse-refinement ladder to $N_J\approx170$ resolves the near-critical case into a monolithic radial spindle, identifying the short beading as a Truelove artefact (Section~\ref{sec:optional_tests}). An exploratory ambipolar-diffusion survey was also run, but it fragments only at $N_J\approx1$--$2$, below the Truelove limit, so it cannot support any conclusion and we exclude it from the argument here; it is described, with an explicit warning that it is numerically unresolved, in the deposited material.

\subsection{Testing the resolution and magnetic-field alternatives}
\label{sec:optional_tests}
Three targeted campaigns test the concerns that most affect the interpretation: whether the longitudinal wavelength is resolution-limited, whether the perpendicular geometry produces genuine short beading, and whether a helical field, the one configuration expected to shorten the axial mode, actually does.

\emph{Resolution stability of $\lambda_{\rm turn}$.} Two refinement directions behave oppositely. Under \emph{transverse} refinement (the isotropic $128^3$--$512^3$ ladder) the short-wavelength spectrum ($\lambda\lesssim0.9\,\lambda_{\rm J}$, $n\gtrsim9$) grows with resolution and is numerical (unresolved transverse sub-fragmentation; Fig.~\ref{fig:growthrate}a), whereas the physical band ($\lambda\gtrsim1\,\lambda_{\rm J}$, $n\leq8$) is converged. Under \emph{axial-only} refinement ($512\times128^2\to1024\times128^2$) the physical band reproduces to better than $0.5\%$ ($\Gamma$ at $n=6,7,8$, i.e.\ $\lambda=1.33,1.14,1.00\,\lambda_{\rm J}$, is $17.6,18.7,18.7$ at both resolutions, plateauing without turning over), \emph{and} the short-$\lambda$ rise is unchanged, confirming it is fixed by the axial Nyquist scale, not a physical mode migrating to shorter $\lambda$. The $\lambda_{\rm turn}\lesssim1\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ upper bound is therefore stable: axial refinement does not move the physical-band plateau, and the only shorter-$\lambda$ structure is the transverse-resolution artefact already identified.

\emph{The perpendicular geometry: a resolved spindle, not short beading.} On a transverse-refinement ladder ($128^3$--$512^3$) the near-critical \emph{ideal} perpendicular filament collapses monolithically in the radial direction at every resolution, the axial mode staying at the box scale and the axial perturbation at seed level throughout the well-resolved phase (at $512^3$ the spine stays resolved with $N_J\approx170$ cells at $C\approx3$, falling only to $N_J\approx40$ at $C\approx30$; still $\approx10\times$ above Truelove; Appendix~\ref{sec:validation}). Short-wavelength axial structure ($\lambda/W\approx0.4$) appears only once the spine drops below $N_J\approx1$; refinement delays its onset to deeper collapse, the signature of a grid artefact. The perpendicular field therefore \emph{suppresses} axial fragmentation in favour of radial collapse, as static linear theory predicts (a perpendicular field lengthens the axial mode), and the short low-resolution beading is numerical. This is for \emph{ideal} MHD; the ambipolar-perpendicular case (Section~\ref{sec:modecompetition}) remains separately unconverged.

\emph{A seeded helical field pinches the width but does not shorten $\lambda_{\rm turn}$; the force-balanced case remains untested.} A divergence-free helical-field scan ($B_\phi/B_z=0$--$2$; construction and scan detail in the deposited material) leaves the axial growth-rate plateau at $\lambda\approx1\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ for every winding and produces only a radial \emph{pinch} that narrows $W_{\rm FWHM}$ by $\approx25\%$ (raising $\lambda/W$ by reducing $W$, not by shortening $\lambda$; the deposited material), so, being seeded rather than force-balanced, it cannot test the confining Fiege--Pudritz sausage ($m=0$) mode \citep{Fiege2000}; the one geometry that could shorten $\lambda$, which therefore remains the single relevant magnetic configuration left unconstrained (Section~\ref{sec:why_mhd_fails}).





\section{The simulated fragmentation scale}
\label{sec:validation_endtoend}

\subsection{What observable does a simulation predict?}
\label{sec:whatobservable}

A simulation that produces one bead per wavelength predicts a \emph{line density}, one core per $\lambda$ of filament. The observable it should be set against is therefore $S_{\rm global}=(L/N)/W$, not the conditional $S_{\rm local}$. The numerical proximity of the simulated bound ($\lesssim1.0$--$1.3$) to $S_{\rm local}\approx1.4$ is therefore not evidence of agreement, since the two are different quantities. Against $S_{\rm global}=3.4$--$5.8$ the simulated bound is in fact \emph{more} discrepant, implying that single-filament fragmentation over-produces cores per unit length unless most of the crest is core-poor, which is the occupancy result of Section~\ref{sec:intermittency}.

\subsection{The controlled experiment}
\label{sec:control}

The load-bearing numerical result is a control, not a campaign. We prepared a drag-relaxed, force-balanced Ostriker cylinder and a dynamically contracting filament and passed both through the \emph{identical} measurement pipeline. For the equilibrium the pipeline recovers the classical maximum at $22H$ ($\lambda_{\rm peak}/22H=1.07$--$1.10$ at two resolutions, a genuinely resolved maximum); for the contracting filament the same pipeline finds no maximum at all within the trustworthy band (Fig.~\ref{fig:eqrecovery}). The difference is a property of the prepared state, not of the estimator.

Wavelengths are measured from the growth-rate spectrum rather than by counting late-time peaks, which is epoch- and resolution-dependent. Seeding twelve axial modes at $\delta v/c_s=10^{-3}$ and fitting the power $P(k,t)$ over its approximately exponential interval gives $\Gamma(\lambda)$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:growthrate}a). It is resolution-converged for $\lambda\gtrsim1\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ ($\Delta\Gamma/\Gamma=3.9$ and $1.1$ per cent between successive resolutions at $n=7$) and rises with transverse resolution below $\lambda\approx0.9\,\lambda_{\rm J}$, which is numerical. Within the converged band $\Gamma$ plateaus rather than turning over, so we quote no wavelength for these runs, only the upper bound $\lambda_{\rm turn}\lesssim1\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ on where a turnover could lie. Forward-modelling through a synthetic beam and the same Plummer fitting used on the data (Eq.~\ref{eq:etaW}) gives the matched bound $S_{\rm sim}^{\rm upper}\lesssim1.0$--$1.3$.

The missing turnover admits two readings that our calculations cannot separate. It may be a resolution limit, with a true maximum below the converged band that would need of order $1024^3$ to expose; or it may be physical, because a filament that contracts while it fragments has no single quasi-static background and a broad plateau is then the expected outcome. It is not a mode-quantisation artefact: the $128^3$--$512^3$ ladder resolves a different maximum axial wavenumber at each resolution, yet the plateau edge does not move. Two caveats temper the causal attribution. The fitted rates ($\Gamma\approx18$--$26\,t_{\rm J}^{-1}$) are the accelerating growth of a collapsing background, not quasi-static eigenvalues, so we use only the mode \emph{ordering}; and a $p=4$ initial profile gives the same order-$\lambda_{\rm J}$ scale, so the deficit is not a profile-index artefact.

\subsection{The contraction ladder}
\label{sec:ladder}

The equilibrium-versus-contracting comparison establishes the sign of the effect but not its dependence on contraction rate, so we ran the ladder. Five otherwise identical filaments were initialised from the same relaxed Ostriker cylinder with a homologous inward velocity $v_r(r)=-A\,(c_s/R_0)\,r$ for $A=0$, $0.25$, $0.5$, $0.75$ and $1$, at $256\times64^2$ with $\Delta x=0.0625\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ and $L_x=16\,\lambda_{\rm J}$, and evolved with the same twelve-mode seed and the same growth-rate measurement.

The prediction to be tested is that mode selection tracks the density at which the modes actually grow, $\lambda_{\rm select}\propto\rho_{\rm eff}^{-1/2}$. Measuring $\rho_{\rm eff}$ as the central density at the mid-point of the linear growth window, the five runs span a factor $4.8$ in $\rho_{\rm eff}$ ($0.68$--$3.24\,\rho_0$) and give
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\rm select}\propto\rho_{\rm eff}^{-0.55\pm0.12},\qquad r=-0.94,
\label{eq:ladderfit}
\end{equation}
consistent with the predicted $-1/2$ (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). The static run recovers the classical value at its own instantaneous density, $\lambda_{\rm peak}/22H(\rho_{\rm eff})=1.09$, while every contracting run falls below it, at $0.79$--$0.94$. The $A=0.25$ run shows no resolved maximum within the seeded modes and is an upper limit. Repeating the two endpoints at doubled resolution ($512\times128^2$) confirms this: $\lambda_{\rm peak}/22H(\rho_{\rm eff})=0.99$ for $A=0$ and $0.84$ for $A=1$, against $1.09$ and $0.94$ at the production resolution.

Two things follow, and the second is as important as the first. The proposed mechanism is confirmed: the wavelength is set by the instantaneous Jeans length at mode selection, and contraction shortens it by raising that density. But the relation between the \emph{initial} contraction rate $A$ and $\rho_{\rm eff}$ is not monotonic, because a strong initial inward velocity overshoots and rebounds, so that the $A=1$ run is at \emph{lower} density during its growth window than the $A=0.25$ run. Contraction rate is therefore not itself the control parameter; $\rho_{\rm eff}$ is. This is a stronger and narrower statement than the two-point control alone supported, and it means the mechanism should be quoted as a dependence on the density at mode selection rather than on how fast the filament is falling in.

\subsection{What the simulations do and do not show}
\label{sec:simsummary}

The result is one-sided. These calculations rule out the assertion that a contracting filament \emph{must} fragment at the equilibrium-cylinder wavelength, and they show that mode selection follows the instantaneous central density. They do not determine a preferred wavelength, and they do not reproduce the observations: the simulated filaments produce a quasi-periodic comb of 3--10 near-uniform beads, whereas the observed cores reject both periodic and Poisson descriptions and occupy only $6$--$27$ per cent of the crest. A successful theory of filament fragmentation must predict a point process, not merely a wavelength. No calculation presented here does so. The numerical work therefore establishes that sub-equilibrium fragmentation scales are dynamically possible; it does not identify the origin of the observed spacing distribution.

One further limitation is thermodynamic. Because $\Gamma(\lambda)$ does not turn over within the converged band, an isothermal calculation contains no small-scale cutoff and cannot predict a characteristic core mass or a CMF peak. That would require $\gamma_{\rm eff}>1$ at $n\gtrsim10^{5}$--$10^{6}\,{\rm cm^{-3}}$, where dust--gas coupling and line cooling can no longer hold $T$ constant \citep{Larson1985,Glover2012}; this is a change in thermal behaviour distinct from, and at much lower density than, the classical opacity limit \citep{Rees1976,LowLyndenBell1976}. Our isothermal suite deliberately excludes it.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the spacing matters: fragment masses and the CMF}
\label{sec:cmf}
The spacing matters because, with the line mass, it sets the characteristic fragment mass that seeds the core mass function (CMF) and ultimately the IMF: for a filament of line mass $M_{\rm line}$ fragmenting at spacing $\lambda$ the mean fragment mass is $M_{\rm frag}\sim\epsilon\,M_{\rm line}\,\lambda$, and a near-critical $M_{\rm line}\approx16\,M_\odot\,{\rm pc^{-1}}$ with $\lambda\approx0.1$--$0.2$\,pc gives $\sim0.5$--$1\,M_\odot$ for $\epsilon\sim0.3$, consistent with the HGBS CMF peak while the classical $5$--$6\times$FWHM would overshoot it. This is only an order-of-magnitude \emph{consistency check}: it is degenerate in $\epsilon M_{\rm line}$ (only the product enters) and yields a \emph{mean}, not a peaked distribution. An \emph{isothermal} EOS lacks a small-scale thermodynamic cutoff and therefore cannot predict a characteristic core mass or a unique CMF peak; only a non-isothermal EOS ($\gamma_{\rm eff}>1$ at $n\gtrsim10^{5}$--$10^{6}\,{\rm cm^{-3}}$) would convert the isothermal plateau into a physical peak (Section~\ref{sec:validation_endtoend}).

\subsection{Hierarchical-fibre projection}
\label{sec:fibre_projection}

A purely geometric route reaches a sub-classical local spacing without modified physics. HGBS filaments are bundles of $N$ velocity-coherent fibres \citep{Hacar2013,Hacar2018,Yang2024}; if each fibre fragments near the classical wavelength but spacing is measured \emph{across} the bundle, as any filament-level pipeline does, the interleaving compresses the apparent spacing. The premise, quasi-periodic fibre-to-core fragmentation at the classical scale, is a hypothesis rather than a measurement (\citealt{Yang2024} find only a weak imprint).

A toy geometrical model that reuses our exact ACS measurement, populating a filament with $N$ cylinders of width $W_{\rm fib}$ each fragmenting at $\lambda_{\rm fib}\approx5$--$6\,W_{\rm fib}$ with random phase and $\sim30$ per cent lognormal scatter, gives in the interleaving limit
\begin{equation}
\frac{\lambda}{W_{\rm fil}}\approx\frac{(5\text{--}6)\,r}{N},\qquad r\equiv W_{\rm fib}/W_{\rm fil},
\label{eq:fourrn}
\end{equation}
verified across $r$ and $N$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:fibre_degen}. Either effect alone suffices: one fibre of half the filament width, or two full-width fibres, both give $\approx2.5$--$3.0$. The observation constrains only the combination $r/N$ ($\approx0.07$--$0.35$ across our conventions) and not $N$ itself, so the relation cannot be inverted for the fibre multiplicity. Since a near-critical fibre would itself fragment sub-classically, the classical-fibre result is an upper bound, and the fibre and single-filament pictures are not independent.

\label{fig:fibre_degen}

Fibre projection is therefore complementary to, not exclusive of, dynamical contraction, and the two are observationally degenerate in the filament-averaged spacing. Breaking the degeneracy needs the tracer that resolves the fibres directly. A third route to the same reconciliation is post-formation dynamical evolution: cores accrete, migrate and interact over several free-fall times \citep{Kirk2016,Mattern2018}, so relaxation of an initially quasi-periodic chain could erase the comb, as \citet{Clarke2020} find. We do not simulate that stage.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{A candidate mechanism for the two-scale pattern.} Short intra-group spacing coexisting with a near-classical filament-averaged line density has an economical explanation in spatially non-uniform seeding: real filaments inherit an inhomogeneous spectrum of density and velocity perturbations from turbulence, accretion and fibre substructure, so fragmentation runs to non-linearity preferentially in perturbation-rich, locally supercritical stretches while low-amplitude stretches stay barren. Uniformly seeded simulations produce a comb rather than this two-scale pattern; the direct test is to seed a near-critical filament with an inhomogeneous perturbation field and ask whether the resulting core chains reproduce the observed occupancy and Fano statistics.

\subsection{Field geometry, boundary conditions and the perpendicular case}
\label{sec:why_mhd_fails}

The common argument that most filaments carry perpendicular fields (from the Planck tendency for dense filaments to lie perpendicular to the large-scale field; \citealt{Planck2016}) uses a projected orientation that need not equal the internal geometry, and hourglass morphologies with the field turning longitudinal inside the crest are observed \citep{Jadhav2026}. A resolved perpendicular filament spindles rather than fragmenting axially (Section~\ref{sec:optional_tests}), so it does not produce the observed axial core chains and is not a source of tension.

Where perpendicular geometry does apply, three non-exclusive resolutions of the residual deficit remain: hierarchical fibre fragmentation (bundles of near-critical fibres projected across the bundle; \citealt{Hacar2013,Hacar2018,Yang2024}); non-ideal MHD at later evolution, which narrows the factor-$\sim3$ perpendicular deficit; and an observational lag in which the cores are relics of an earlier episode. These are separated by the future tests listed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\emph{The untested magnetic configuration.} The scan constrains only the initial field geometries simulated (Table~\ref{tab:magcases}). A uniform longitudinal field was tested in the adopted non-equilibrium configuration and leaves the axial mode essentially unchanged. For a static perpendicular field the apparent short fragmentation is resolution-sensitive and is not established. A seeded toroidal component pinches the filament but is not a stability calculation for a helical equilibrium. The force-balanced Fiege--Pudritz helical/toroidal equilibrium, which is the one configuration that could genuinely shorten the axial mode, is untested here. Magnetic tension is therefore neither favoured nor disfavoured by these calculations: no force-balanced helical equilibrium was simulated, so the campaign places no constraint on this channel. For scale only, and read off from the published \citet{Fiege2000} results rather than computed here, a strongly toroidally dominated equilibrium shortens the $m=0$ wavelength by up to a factor $\approx2$; our $\beta\approx0.3$--$2$ places these filaments at the weak end of that sequence, so such a field could be astrophysically significant, though its magnitude cannot be assessed without a force-balanced calculation matched to the observed filament parameters. Settling it needs a validated cylindrical MHD eigensolver rather than the algebraic relation of Section~\ref{sec:magnetic}: one would construct a Fiege--Pudritz self-similar equilibrium with poloidal $B_z(r)$ and toroidal $B_\phi(r)$ in radial force balance, confirm it is stationary by drag-relaxing and then evolving drag-free exactly as in our equilibrium control, and only then seed the $m=0$ mode. That is a paper in itself.



\emph{The infall connection.} A dynamically fragmenting filament fragments below the equilibrium wavelength only while it is still contracting, so the interpretation ultimately rests on the filaments' accretion state. Per-cloud infall-velocity or mass-growth-rate constraints that would place Orion~B, Aquila, Perseus and Taurus filaments inside or outside the simulated non-equilibrium range are not established for these specific clouds, so the simulation--cloud correspondence is a plausibility argument, not a demonstrated match.

Sub-isothermal equations of state ($\gamma<1$) accelerate fragmentation by $30$--$40\%$ without changing the wavelength, while adiabatic heating ($\gamma=5/3$) suppresses it entirely (Appendix~\ref{sec:validation}). The isothermal suite is therefore the conservative case in a globally averaged sense; though not locally, where a $\sim12\%$ dust-temperature variation shifts the local $\lambda/W$ by $\sim10$\,per-cent and relocates where cores form (Section~\ref{sec:mt_variation}).

\subsubsection{Non-Ideal MHD Effects}
\label{sec:nonideal}

At the densities probed here ($n_0\sim10^4$\,cm$^{-3}$), the ambipolar-diffusion time ($\sim10$--$20\,t_{\rm ff}$; \citealt{Tassis2004}) is long compared with the collapse time, so ideal MHD is a good approximation for the longitudinal fragmentation stage: four non-ideal runs at $\eta_{\rm AD}=\{0.01,0.05\}$ still collapse radially, $\sim12$--$15\%$ \textit{earlier} than the ideal control (flux leakage erodes magnetic support, the conservative direction). Non-ideal effects nonetheless change the outcome for perpendicular fields, where ambipolar diffusion promotes longitudinal fragmentation (Section~\ref{sec:modecompetition}).

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Four limitations bound the conclusions.

\emph{Sample.} Four nearby, low-mass Gould Belt regions of broadly solar metallicity and similar radiation field are not a population. Whether the result persists in high-mass complexes, at lower metallicity or under stronger irradiation is untested.

\emph{Method.} Two validation tests are outstanding. The $\lesssim5$ per cent bound on manual skeleton editing is a proxy, set by the junction-retention and association-radius sensitivities rather than by a blind multi-editor re-skeletonisation; and the small-separation deficit cannot be separated into instrumental and physical parts without a faint-segment injection--recovery experiment at the map level. All code and catalogues are deposited (Data Availability) so that both can be carried out.

\emph{Observations not available to us.} A per-segment line-mass split, which would test whether the most supercritical filaments show the shortest spacing, needs segment-level line masses at a fidelity the data do not support; resolved velocity dispersions per stretch, which would say why some eligible filament forms cores and some does not, do not exist for these regions.

\emph{Simulations.} These are idealised controlled experiments, not models of specific filaments. They are isothermal, use simplified initial profiles and a prescribed rather than driven turbulent spectrum, and the transverse boundaries are an idealisation of ambient confinement. The perpendicular-field and ambipolar parameter space is not numerically resolved at the resolutions we could afford ($N_J\approx1$--$2$, below the Truelove requirement) and therefore supplies no constraint on the fragmentation wavelength; those runs are excluded from the scientific argument and retained only in the deposited material.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{A recommendation for observers.} Because the skeleton systematic is the dominant limit on any comparison with theory, we recommend that filament skeletons be extracted to a fixed column-density contrast relative to the local background rather than an absolute level, and that spacings be quoted for \emph{both} a persistent-crest and a medial-axis definition so that the factor-of-two ontological span is stated rather than hidden.



\subsection{Non-isothermal effects and the temperature-gradient test}
\label{sec:mt_variation}

The MHD suite is isothermal, whereas HGBS dust-temperature maps vary by $\sim12$\,per-cent ($\sim10$--$13$\,K), a local perturbation since $\lambda_{\rm J}\propto\sqrt{T}$. A single illustrative run with an imposed $\sim12$\,per-cent longitudinal temperature gradient fragments \emph{differentially} (the cool, locally supercritical half beads while the warm half is suppressed), shifting the local wavelength by $\sim10$\,per-cent. This is a single illustrative run, not a converged parameter study, and we quote it as an indication of the direction and rough size of the effect only; temperature sets both the Jeans scale and the critical line mass ($\mu_{\rm crit}\propto T$). The isothermal assumption is thus conservative only in a globally averaged sense; a self-consistent treatment is deferred to future work.

\section{Conclusions}

We have re-measured core spacing along filament crests in four HGBS clouds with \textit{Gaia} DR3 distances, and complemented the measurement with a controlled numerical experiment. Our conclusions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item \textbf{There is no single observational core spacing to compare with a one-wavelength theory.} The quantity such a theory predicts is the unconditional filament length per core, $S_{\rm global}$, which is $3.4$, $3.4$, $4.5$ and $5.8$ in Orion~B, Aquila, Taurus and Perseus: essentially classical in Perseus, close to it in Taurus, and below the classical band by less than a factor of two in the other two. Measured only within core-bearing stretches, $S_{\rm local}$ is several times shorter.
\item \textbf{The difference between them is the result.} Cores occupy only $6$--$27$ per cent of the crest, forming tight groups separated by long bare stretches. Core formation along these filaments is therefore spatially intermittent, and what a fragmentation model must reproduce is a point process, not a spacing.
\item \textbf{The all-pairs median is the wrong estimator of local spacing.} It converges to $\approx0.293$ times the extent of whatever segment it is applied to. We use adjacent along-crest separations instead and retain the all-pairs statistic only for historical comparison.
\item \textbf{The magnitude of the local deficit is dominated by the choice of structural ontology, not by physics.} Changing from the persistent-crest network to a mask medial axis moves $S_{\rm local}$ by a factor of two and $S_{\rm global}$ by more. The \emph{sign} of the deficit is robust; the magnitude is not. Persistence thresholding within one ontology changes it by less than ten per cent (Section~\ref{sec:persistence}), so this is a question of definition rather than of parameter tuning, and it will be settled by velocity-resolved rather than image-plane data.
\item \textbf{The cores are neither periodic nor Poisson, but the smallest-separation deficit is not shown to be physical.} Both models are rejected in all four clouds, and the rejections survive a moving-block bootstrap. No classical-scale periodicity is detected in the pair-correlation function at the $95$ per cent level. The inferred exclusion scale, $0.011$--$0.044$\,pc, lies entirely within the \textit{Herschel} beam and \textsc{getsources} deblending limit, so it cannot be claimed as Jeans-scale suppression until map-level pair-injection tests are performed.
\item \textbf{The inhomogeneity is largely inherited from the filament.} Against a null conditioned on local column density we detect excess clumping in one cloud (Taurus) and fail to detect it in three. That null is conservative by construction, so the three non-detections are upper limits rather than evidence of absence, and with $N=4$ neither statement generalises to a population.
\item \textbf{The eligible-length diagnostic is exploratory.} Restricting to column-density-selected supercritical crest moves the two statistics together, but the criterion is partly endogenous and the correction is robust in only two clouds. None of the conclusions above depends on it.
\item \textbf{The simulations are a counterexample, not an explanation.} A contracting filament is not constrained to fragment at the equilibrium wavelength; the identical pipeline recovers the classical value for a relaxed equilibrium cylinder but finds no resolved maximum for a contracting one, so only an upper bound $S_{\rm sim}^{\rm upper}\lesssim1.0$--$1.3$ is available. A five-point ladder in contraction rate shows that mode selection tracks the density at which the modes grow, $\lambda_{\rm select}\propto\rho_{\rm eff}^{-0.55\pm0.12}$, close to the predicted $-1/2$. The simulations nevertheless produce a quasi-periodic comb and do not reproduce the observed intermittent point process, so they do not identify the origin of the observed spacing distribution.
\item \textbf{The physical cause is undetermined.} Dynamical contraction, hierarchical-fibre projection and helical magnetic tension all remain viable. The first is tested here, the second treated only geometrically, and the third not tested at all: no force-balanced helical equilibrium was simulated, so our campaign places no constraint whatever on the magnetic channel, which is mentioned for completeness rather than as a coequal candidate.
\end{enumerate}

The decisive next observations are velocity-resolved: dense-gas kinematics that identify which skeleton corresponds to a coherent physical object, and resolved velocity dispersions per stretch that would say why some supercritical filament forms cores and some does not.

\section{The $L/3$ bias of the pairwise-median spacing}
\label{sec:pwmedian}
For $N$ points uniformly distributed on a filament of length $L$, a single pairwise distance $D=|X_i-X_j|$ has the triangular PDF $f(d)=2(L-d)/L^2$, with mean $\langle D\rangle=L/3$ and median $d^\star=L(1-1/\sqrt{2})\approx0.293\,L\approx0.88\,(L/3)$. The median of all $N(N-1)/2$ pairwise distances converges to $d^\star$ as $N\to\infty$, for a random or a perfectly periodic distribution alike, so the pairwise median measures the region extent, not the fragmentation wavelength. A Monte-Carlo over $N=5$--$500$ gives median$/L\approx0.293\,(1+0.43/N)$, i.e.\ $\le2\%$ inflation for $N\gtrsim20$, so the convergence is reached at the core counts of interest. For branched skeletons the statistic measures $\approx0.3\times$ the component's arclength extent, and for a realistic filament length distribution it tracks the rms-length$/3$ (full tables in the Zenodo archive). Computing the pairwise median over \emph{all} cores in each primary region (without per-filament segmentation) returns $\sim L_{\rm region}/3$ ($12.5$, $7.5$, $8.0$, $2.5$\,pc for Orion~B, Aquila, Perseus, Taurus), the region extent, confirming directly that the statistic measures segmentation scale rather than the fragmentation wavelength.

\section{Validation of the measurement pipeline}
\label{sec:validation}

The measurement chain was validated end to end before being applied. Rather than reproduce the individual tests here, we summarise them and note that the scripts and outputs are deposited with the code (Data Availability).

\emph{Closed-loop injection--recovery.} Synthetic core chains of known spacing were injected onto the real skeletons and recovered through the full pipeline; the recovered $\lambda/W$ matches the injected value to $0.4$ per cent over $\lambda/W=1.4$--$5.7$. A periodic injection over-corrects for a clustered process, which is why we quote raw values in the main text and carry the correction only as a bracket (Section~\ref{sec:injection_recovery}).

\emph{Eigenvalue check of the classical benchmark.} The dispersion relation of the isothermal equilibrium cylinder was solved from scratch and agrees with \citet{Nagasawa1987} and \citet{Fischera2012} to better than $1$ per cent, peaking at $\lambda_{\rm IM92}\approx2.3\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ with a cutoff at $\lambda_{\rm cr}\approx1.2\,\lambda_{\rm J}$. The measured contracting-filament spectrum instead rises monotonically into the grid-scale region, which is the basis for quoting an upper bound.

\emph{Width normalisation.} Forward-modelling the simulation output through a synthetic \textit{Herschel} beam and the same Plummer fitting used on the data gives $\eta_W=T_1=0.59$--$0.78$, with $W_{\rm form}=0.683\pm0.012\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ converged to $0.9$ per cent across resolutions. This is Eq.~\ref{eq:etaW}, the only route by which a simulated wavelength is compared with an observed spacing.

\emph{Resolution.} The load-bearing runs satisfy the Truelove criterion at the spacing-setting epoch ($N_J\approx5$--$7$), which is above the $N_J=4$ floor but not far above it, and this is one reason we quote a bound rather than a value. The growth-rate spectrum is resolution-converged for $\lambda\gtrsim1\,\lambda_{\rm J}$ at $128^3$--$512^3$ and separates only at the grid scale (Fig.~\ref{fig:growthrate}a).

\section{Specification of the inhomogeneous null and the freeze-out argument}
\label{sec:nullspec}

\emph{The column-density-conditioned null.} The null used in Sections~\ref{sec:intermittency} and \ref{sec:clustering} asks whether cores are clumped \emph{beyond} the clumping already present in the filament they lie on. It is an inhomogeneous Poisson process on the one-dimensional skeleton with intensity
\begin{equation}
\lambda(x)\;\propto\;\left[\max\!\left(N_{\rm H_2}(x)-N_0,\,0\right)\right]^{\alpha},
\label{eq:nullintensity}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is arclength along the spine, $N_{\rm H_2}(x)$ the column density of the skeleton pixel at $x$, $N_0=10^{21}\,{\rm cm^{-2}}$ a floor that prevents low-column pixels from dominating, and $\alpha$ a single exponent fitted per sample. The normalisation is fixed by requiring $\int\lambda\,{\rm d}x=N_{\rm obs}$, so each realisation contains the observed number of cores. The exponent is fitted by maximum likelihood on the observed core positions, and, to avoid the circularity of fitting and testing on the same data, we refit it under leave-one-cloud-out cross-validation: for each cloud, $\alpha$ is estimated from the other three and applied to the held-out cloud. The fitted values are $\alpha=1.6$--$2.4$ with a cross-validated mean of $2.0$, and the conclusions are unchanged over $\alpha=1$--$3$.

For each cloud we draw $10^4$ realisations, compute the same Fano factor $F(1\,{\rm pc})$ and pair-correlation $g(s)$ estimators used on the data, and quote the empirical $p$-value $(1+\#\{F_{\rm null}\geq F_{\rm obs}\})/(1+N_{\rm MC})$ and the pointwise $2.5$th--$97.5$th percentile envelope. Two properties of this null should be kept in mind. It is conditional on the deposited skeleton, so it tests the distribution of cores along a fixed network and not the network itself. And it is \emph{conservative}: the column density entering Eq.~\ref{eq:nullintensity} is the observed map, which the cores themselves raise, so part of the signal being tested for is built into the null. That is the same endogeneity discussed in Section~\ref{sec:eligible}, and it biases the test against detecting excess clumping. Non-detections should therefore be read as upper limits on excess clumping rather than as evidence of its absence.

\emph{Why a contracting filament fragments below the equilibrium wavelength.} The numerical result of Section~\ref{sec:sims} has a simple WKB reading, which we give here because it is the reason we regard the counterexample as generic rather than accidental. In a filament of central density $\rho_c(t)$ the local Jeans length is $\lambda_{\rm J}(t)=c_s\sqrt{\pi/G\rho_c(t)}$, and an axial mode of fixed comoving wavelength grows at a rate $\Gamma(\lambda,t)$ that is positive only for $\lambda\gtrsim\lambda_{\rm J}(t)$. A mode is realised as a fragment only if it has time to grow before the background changes, that is if
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\rm grow}(\lambda)\;\equiv\;\Gamma(\lambda,t)^{-1}\;\lesssim\;\tau_{\rm contract}\;\equiv\;\left|\frac{\rm d\ln\rho_c}{{\rm d}t}\right|^{-1}.
\label{eq:freezeout}
\end{equation}
For an equilibrium cylinder $\tau_{\rm contract}\to\infty$ and every unstable mode has unlimited time, so the mode that dominates is the one with the largest $\Gamma$, the classical $\lambda_{\rm m}\approx22H$. For a contracting filament $\tau_{\rm contract}$ is of order the free-fall time and $\lambda_{\rm J}(t)$ falls as $\rho_c^{-1/2}$, so long-wavelength modes, whose growth times are the longest, do not complete before the state they were computed for has gone. The surviving modes are those with $\tau_{\rm grow}\lesssim\tau_{\rm contract}$, and since $\Gamma$ increases towards shorter $\lambda$ over the converged band this selects the short end. The fragmentation scale is then set by the competition in Eq.~\ref{eq:freezeout} rather than by the maximum of $\Gamma$, which is why no turnover appears and why only an upper bound $\lambda_{\rm turn}$ can be quoted. The same argument predicts that the effect should weaken smoothly as the initial contraction rate is reduced towards zero, which is the ladder of Section~\ref{sec:ladder}.
